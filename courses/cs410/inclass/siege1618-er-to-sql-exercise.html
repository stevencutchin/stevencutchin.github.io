In-class exercise · one session

Siege 1618:
design document → database

You have been handed a game design document. It is informal, incomplete, and ambiguous in exactly the places that matter. This is what a real specification looks like.

By the end of the session you will have taken it through the whole pipeline — ER model, relational schema, DDL, and the queries the team will actually run against it — and you will have had to make three design decisions the document does not make for you.

The dashed arrow is the part most courses leave out — and the part you will feel most sharply today.

The mini-world

What the document gives you

Siege 1618 is a two-player, two-screen siege game. Each player holds a terrain map, raises a castle, harvests resources, trains units, and attacks the other side.

Stated in the document

  • Resources — wood, stone, crops, gold. Each sits at a tile position and has an amount remaining. Serfs harvest them.
  • Units — serf, archer, cannon. Each has a position, hit points, attack damage, speed in tiles per second, and a range.
  • Structures — castle, tower, wall. Each has a position, hit points, attack damage, rate of fire, and a range.
  • Terrain — a map of tiles, some holding resources, some open plain. Shipped as a level file.
  • Production — a structure can be told to produce a serf or an archer.

Not stated — and you must decide

  • There are players and there are matches. The document never mentions either. Nothing in it tells you who owns a unit or which game it is in.
  • Attack damage and speed are listed as properties of a unit. Are they really?
  • An attack has an attacker and a target. Either one can be a unit or a structure.
  • Nothing records history — only current state. Can you answer "how much damage did she deal last match?" from that?

Decision 1 of 3 · worked for you

"Speed: tiles per second" — whose property is that?

The document lists speed, attack and range under Units. Read it literally and you get one entity, UNIT, carrying all of them.

But every archer in the game moves at the same speed. That number belongs to archers, not to this archer. Store it per instance and you have written the same value into ten thousand rows, and a balance patch that nerfs archer speed becomes a mass update that can go half-finished.

The split

UNIT_TYPE holds what is true of every archer: max HP, attack, speed, range, build cost. UNIT holds what is true of this one: which type it is, where it stands, its current HP, who owns it, when it died.

Two different clocks. Anything on the left changes when a designer edits a balance file; anything on the right changes during play.

Part 1 · your turn

Entities, attributes, keys 10 min

Task 1.1

Name your entity types

Apply the type/instance split from the last slide across the whole document. Expect somewhere between nine and thirteen entity types — if you have four, you have collapsed something; if you have twenty-five, you have promoted an attribute.

  1. For each entity type, list its attributes and underline the primary key. Mark any composite, multivalued or derived attributes with the Chen notation from the Ch. 3 lecture.
  2. Two of your entity types will have no natural key in the document. Say what you are using instead and why.
  3. Position is a trap. X and Y appear on resources, on units, and on structures. Decide: is position a composite attribute, or is a tile an entity in its own right? Write down one sentence justifying your choice — you will be held to it in Part 2.

Prompt if you are stuck

Ask of every noun in the document: would I ever need to ask a question about this thing on its own? If yes, it is probably an entity. If it only ever appears as a detail of something else, it is an attribute.

Part 1 · your turn

Relationships and cardinality 10 min

Task 1.2

Connect them, and label every edge

Every relationship gets a cardinality ratio and a participation constraint. Say both out loud in each direction before you write them down.

  1. A player and a match. What ratio? What does the relationship itself need to carry that neither entity can hold?
  2. A serf and a resource node. A serf harvests the same node repeatedly, and a node is worked by many serfs over a match. What does that force, and what does the relationship carry?
  3. A structure and the units it produces.
  4. A level and its terrain tiles. Does a tile have an identity of its own, independent of the level it sits in?
  5. A match and a level. Which direction is total?

Watch for

Two of the relationships above have an attribute that belongs to the relationship and not to either entity. If you cannot find them, you have probably hidden them inside one of the entities — which will cost you in Part 4 when you try to total them up.

Decision 2 of 3 · guided

Does a tile exist without its level?

Tile (12, 7) is meaningless on its own. There is a (12, 7) on every level in the game, and they are different places. The tile is identified only within a level.

That is the definition of a weak entity: no key of its own, identified by a partial key plus the key of an owner, through an identifying relationship with total participation.

Partial key

The pair (X, Y) is the partial key of TERRAIN_TILE. It becomes a full key only once LevelID is carried in from LEVEL. Draw it with the double rectangle and the double diamond.

Now the harder half

A resource node sits at a tile. A unit stands at a tile. Should those also be relationships to TERRAIN_TILE, or should units and resources just carry X and Y as plain attributes?

Argue it both ways in your pair, then commit. The honest answer depends on a question nobody has asked you yet: does anything need to query by tile? Hold that thought until Part 4 — question Q2 will settle it.

This is the dashed arrow from slide 1. You cannot finish the model without knowing the workload, and you rarely know the workload before you have a model. Real projects iterate; you are iterating in one class period.

Decision 3 of 3 · the interesting one

Who attacked whom?

An archer shoots a wall. A tower shoots a serf. A cannon shells a castle. The attacker may be a unit or a structure; so may the target.

You need to record attacks — the design document asks for damage numbers and the team will want to know who dealt them. So you need a relationship whose endpoints are "a unit or a structure."

ER notation has no such thing. A relationship connects named entity types. This is the moment where a clean diagram meets an awkward reality, and there are three standard answers.

Why it matters more than it looks

Every telemetry question the designers will ask — damage by player, kill attribution, which tower is doing the work — runs through this one relationship. Choose badly and those queries become four-way unions.

The three candidates

  1. Nullable foreign-key pairs. One ATTACK relation with AttackerUnitID, AttackerStructureID, TargetUnitID, TargetStructureID; exactly one of each pair is non-null.
  2. Split the relation. Four relations — unit-on-unit, unit-on-structure, structure-on-unit, structure-on-structure.
  3. A shared supertype. Generalize UNIT and STRUCTURE into GAME_OBJECT; ATTACK points at GAME_OBJECT twice.

The next slide lets you see what each one does to the schema and to the query.

Interactive · compare before you commit

Three ways to record an attack

Each design is correct. They differ in what the DBMS can enforce for you, and in what the most common query costs. Pick one and read the query it produces.

Design A, nullable foreign-key pairs. One ATTACK relation with four optional foreign keys. The database cannot enforce 'exactly one of each pair is non-null' with a foreign key alone — it takes a CHECK constraint, and most of the integrity burden falls on application code. Totaling damage per player means coalescing two columns and joining twice.

The schema it produces

ATTACK(AttackID, AttackerUnitID → UNIT, AttackerStructID → STRUCTURE, TargetUnitID → UNIT, TargetStructID → STRUCTURE, Damage, OccurredAt) CHECK (exactly one attacker FK is non-null) CHECK (exactly one target FK is non-null)
ATTACK_U_ON_U(AttackID, AttackerID → UNIT, TargetID → UNIT, Damage, OccurredAt) ATTACK_U_ON_S(AttackID, AttackerID → UNIT, TargetID → STRUCTURE, Damage, OccurredAt) ATTACK_S_ON_U(AttackID, AttackerID → STRUCTURE, TargetID → UNIT, Damage, OccurredAt) ATTACK_S_ON_S(AttackID, AttackerID → STRUCTURE, TargetID → STRUCTURE, Damage, OccurredAt)
GAME_OBJECT(ObjectID, MatchID → MATCH, OwnerID → PLAYER, X, Y, CurrentHP, CreatedAt, DestroyedAt) UNIT(ObjectID → GAME_OBJECT, TypeCode → UNIT_TYPE) STRUCTURE(ObjectID → GAME_OBJECT, TypeCode → STRUCTURE_TYPE) ATTACK(AttackID, AttackerID → GAME_OBJECT, TargetID → GAME_OBJECT, Damage, OccurredAt)

"Total damage dealt by each player in match 7"

SELECT p.Handle, SUM(a.Damage) FROM ATTACK a LEFT JOIN UNIT u ON a.AttackerUnitID = u.UnitID LEFT JOIN STRUCTURE s ON a.AttackerStructID = s.StructureID JOIN PLAYER p ON p.PlayerID = COALESCE(u.OwnerID, s.OwnerID) WHERE COALESCE(u.MatchID, s.MatchID) = 7 GROUP BY p.Handle;

Two outer joins and a COALESCE to recover one owner.

SELECT p.Handle, SUM(d.Damage) FROM ( SELECT AttackerID AS aid, Damage FROM ATTACK_U_ON_U UNION ALL SELECT AttackerID, Damage FROM ATTACK_U_ON_S ) d JOIN UNIT u ON u.UnitID = d.aid JOIN PLAYER p ON p.PlayerID = u.OwnerID WHERE u.MatchID = 7 GROUP BY p.Handle; -- and the same again for the two structure relations

Half the answer. The structure attacks need a second, parallel query unioned on.

SELECT p.Handle, SUM(a.Damage) FROM ATTACK a JOIN GAME_OBJECT g ON g.ObjectID = a.AttackerID JOIN PLAYER p ON p.PlayerID = g.OwnerID WHERE g.MatchID = 7 GROUP BY p.Handle;

Two inner joins, no nulls, no union. Every foreign key is enforced by the DBMS.

Part 2 · your turn

Map it to relations 15 min

Task 2.1

Run the mapping algorithm on your own diagram

Use the seven-step algorithm from Ch. 9. Write each relation as NAME(key, attr, FK → TARGET).

  1. Map your regular entity types. Map your weak entity — remember the owner's key joins the partial key to form the primary key.
  2. Map your relationships. For each 1:N, state which side absorbed the foreign key and why. For each M:N, name the cross-reference relation and say what relationship attributes it carries.
  3. Handle the multivalued attribute if you have one. (If you do not think you have one, look again at a player's resource stockpile across four resource types.)
  4. If you chose the supertype design on slide 9, map the specialization. Which of the four EER mapping options from the Ch. 4 lecture did you use, and is your specialization total or partial, disjoint or overlapping?

Before you move on

Count your relations. Count your foreign keys. Every foreign key should name the relation it points at — if you have a column you cannot trace to a primary key somewhere, that is a bug, not a shortcut.

Compare — then we all work from this

The reference schema

One defensible answer, taking the supertype design. Hold yours next to it: where you differ, decide whether you were wrong or merely different.

PLAYER(PlayerID, Handle, JoinedOn) LEVEL(LevelID, Name, Width, Height) TERRAIN_TILE(LevelID → LEVEL, X, Y, TerrainType) RESOURCE_NODE(NodeID, LevelID → LEVEL, X, Y, ResourceType, InitialAmount) MATCH(MatchID, LevelID → LEVEL, StartedAt, EndedAt) PLAYS_IN(MatchID → MATCH, PlayerID → PLAYER, Side, FinalScore, Outcome) UNIT_TYPE(TypeCode, Name, MaxHP, Attack, Speed, Range, CostWood, CostStone, CostCrops, CostGold) STRUCTURE_TYPE(TypeCode, Name, MaxHP, Attack, RateOfFire, Range, CostWood, CostStone, CostCrops, CostGold) GAME_OBJECT(ObjectID, MatchID → MATCH, OwnerID → PLAYER, X, Y, CurrentHP, CreatedAt, DestroyedAt) UNIT(ObjectID → GAME_OBJECT, TypeCode → UNIT_TYPE) STRUCTURE(ObjectID → GAME_OBJECT, TypeCode → STRUCTURE_TYPE) STOCKPILE(MatchID, PlayerID, ResourceType, Amount) FK (MatchID, PlayerID) → PLAYS_IN HARVEST(HarvestID, ObjectID → UNIT, NodeID → RESOURCE_NODE, Amount, HarvestedAt) ATTACK(AttackID, AttackerID → GAME_OBJECT, TargetID → GAME_OBJECT, Damage, OccurredAt) PRODUCTION_ORDER(OrderID, StructureID → STRUCTURE, TypeCode → UNIT_TYPE, OrderedAt, CompletedAt)

Three things to notice

DestroyedAt and CompletedAt are null while the thing is alive or pending — that is how current state and history live in one relation. STOCKPILE is the multivalued attribute, mapped out. And PLAYS_IN carries Side, FinalScore and Outcome, none of which belong to a player or to a match alone.

Part 3 · your turn

Write the DDL 10 min

Task 3.1

Three CREATE TABLE statements

Write DDL for TERRAIN_TILE, PLAYS_IN and ATTACK. They were chosen because each one forces a different decision.

  • TERRAIN_TILE — a composite primary key that includes a foreign key.
  • PLAYS_IN — a cross-reference relation with its own attributes, and a domain constraint on Outcome.
  • ATTACK — two foreign keys into the same relation, which means you must name the constraints.

Checklist for each table

  • Sensible types and widths — not VARCHAR(255) for everything.
  • NOT NULL wherever participation is total.
  • Primary key, and any additional UNIQUE the cardinality demands.
  • Foreign keys with a deliberate ON DELETE action. Deleting a match should not silently orphan its attacks.
  • A CHECK where a domain is genuinely restricted — Damage >= 0, Outcome IN (…).

Think about

Can a CHECK constraint stop an archer from attacking a target in a different match? Try to write it. What does the attempt tell you about where that rule has to live instead?

Part 4 · the questions the team actually asks

Tier 1 — reading the catalog 6 min

These are the balance-designer's questions. One or two relations, no grouping yet.

  1. Q1

    List every unit type with a range of 4 or more, showing name, attack and speed, cheapest first by gold cost. Single relation · WHERE · ORDER BY

  2. Q2

    For the level named 'Highland Pass', how many tiles are there of each terrain type? Join · GROUP BY — and the query that settles the tile question from slide 7

  3. Q3

    List all gold nodes on that same level, richest first, with their tile coordinates. Join · WHERE on two conditions · ORDER BY DESC

  4. Q4

    Which structure types can attack at all, and what is the ratio of their attack to their rate of fire? Computed column · a thought about division by zero

Before you type

For each one, say out loud which relations it touches. If a query needs a relation your Part 2 schema does not have, write that down — it is the most valuable thing you will produce today.

Part 4 · continued

Tier 2 — match telemetry 10 min

  1. Q5

    In match 7, how many units does each player still have alive? Three-way join · GROUP BY · the NULL test that means "not dead"

  2. Q6

    Total damage dealt by each player in match 7, highest first. Join through the attacker · SUM · this is the query from slide 9

  3. Q7

    For every unit type in the catalog, how many have ever been built across all matches — including types that have never been built at all. Outer join · the zero-row trap: an inner join silently drops the answer you most wanted

  4. Q8

    The five resource nodes worked hardest across the whole database: number of harvest events and total amount taken. GROUP BY · two aggregates · LIMIT

  5. Q9

    Every player's record — matches played, won, and lost. Conditional aggregation: SUM over a CASE, or COUNT of a filtered expression

  6. Q10

    Which players hold more than 500 gold in any match currently in progress? Join STOCKPILE · a NULL test on the match end · HAVING or WHERE — decide which and know why

Part 4 · the hard half

Tier 3 — the designer's questions 9 min

These are the ones that get asked in a balance meeting. Each needs a subquery, an anti-join, or a window function.

  1. Q11

    Damage per gold spent, by unit type, across all matches. Which type is the outlier the designers need to look at? Aggregate divided by a catalog value · ORDER BY an expression

  2. Q12

    Win rate by starting side. Is one side of the map favored? Conditional aggregation as a percentage — the question that ships a patch

  3. Q13

    Units that dealt damage but never took any. NOT EXISTS or a left-join-is-null anti-join · say which one you expect to be faster and why

  4. Q14

    Average time from the start of a match to that player's first completed castle. A correlated MIN · timestamp arithmetic · an aggregate over an aggregate

  5. Q15

    For each match, the structure that stood longest before it was destroyed. Greatest-n-per-group: a window function, or a correlated subquery · what do you do about structures still standing?

  6. Q16

    Players whose average final score beats the average final score of all players. Nested aggregation · the classic "aggregate of an aggregate" that cannot be written in one GROUP BY

Graduate section

CS 510 addendum

Task G.1

Relational algebra

Express Q5, Q7 and Q13 in relational algebra as well as SQL. For Q7, name the operator that does the work an outer join does, and say what the algebra gives you for the types with no units built.

Task G.2

Justify the attack design

You have three candidate designs from slide 9 and a stated workload: Q6, Q13 and Q15 run on every match-end screen. Argue for one design against that workload. Your answer must name what each alternative costs you — in enforced integrity, in query complexity, and in what happens when a third kind of attacker is added next release.

Task G.3

The schema that already exists

The rendering client was written before any of this and reads a flat level file: one row per tile, with resource type and amount folded into the tile row, and no notion of a match.

You may not change it — it ships on consoles. Specify the proxy layer that lets the client keep reading its shape while the server writes to yours: which views, which materialized views, which bridge tables. Justify each choice against the workload above.

Note

G.3 is a miniature of the integration exercise that closes the course. Both schemas stay intact; the proxy layer is the deliverable, and the justification is what gets graded.

Wrap-up

Debrief

The three decisions

  • Type vs. instance. Attack and speed describe archers, not this archer. Getting this wrong costs you a mass update on every balance patch and makes Q11 unanswerable.
  • Weak entities. A tile has no identity without its level. Whether units point at tiles depends on whether anything queries by tile — and Q2 is the thing that does.
  • Polymorphic endpoints. ER has no "unit or structure." You buy enforced integrity with a supertype and pay for it with a join.

The question to take away

Which pair's Part 2 schema made Part 4 shortest? Why? That relationship — workload shaping schema — is most of what database design is in practice.

Hand in

  1. ER diagram, with every edge labeled for cardinality and participation.
  2. Relational schema, keys underlined, foreign keys named with their targets.
  3. DDL for TERRAIN_TILE, PLAYS_IN and ATTACK.
  4. A .sql file with as many of Q1–Q16 as you reached, in order, each preceded by the question as a comment.
  5. One paragraph: the query you could not write against your own Part 2 schema, and what you would change.

Item 5 is worth as much as the rest. Finding the question your model cannot answer is the skill; writing the model that answers it was never going to happen on the first try.
