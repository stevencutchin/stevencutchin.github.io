Query processing · what the engine actually does

A range query is
a descent and a walk

A WHERE clause with a range in it looks like a test the engine has to apply to every row. It isn't. With a B+tree it is three motions: fall to the bottom of the tree at the low end, walk forward along the leaves, and stop one comparison past the high end.

The reason it works is the same property that made the join cheap: the leaf level of a B+tree is a sorted linked list. A range is a contiguous stretch of that list. Find where it starts, and the rest is reading forward until you fall off the end of the range.

Sorted order is what turns a test into a window.

Motivation

Why this is worth an hour

Range predicates are most of what real WHERE clauses contain — dates, prices, scores, timestamps. Nearly every "why is this query slow" question is about one.

  • You will be able to explain why an index was ignored. A perfectly good index gets skipped when the range is wide, and that is the optimizer being right, not broken.
  • You will know which column order to index. Range scans are why a composite index on (a, b) is not the same thing as one on (b, a).
  • You will recognize the mistakes that silently disable an index — wrapping the column in a function is the classic one, and it turns a walk back into a full scan.

The claim we are going to defend

Answering a range predicate from a B+tree costs one root-to-leaf descent plus a forward walk of exactly the matching stretch, plus a single extra comparison to learn where to stop. The work is proportional to the size of the answer, not the size of the table.

And the catch

That is only a win while the answer is small. When the range covers much of the table, walking the index and fetching rows one at a time is slower than reading the whole table sequentially. The crossover is the interesting part, and the demonstrator later makes you watch it happen.

The running example

One table, one range

Our UNIVERSITY mini-world, trimmed to twelve students so the whole thing fits on a slide. There is a B+tree index on GPA.

STUDENT(StudentID, Name, Major, GPA) INDEX student_gpa_idx ON STUDENT(GPA)

SELECT StudentID, Name, GPA FROM STUDENT WHERE GPA BETWEEN 3.05 AND 3.50;

Two things to notice now

The table is listed in GPA order here for our convenience, but it is not stored that way — rows sit on disk in whatever order they were inserted. The index is the only thing that knows the sorted order.

And the shape of the answer

Five rows out of twelve. In GPA order they are adjacent: once you find 3.05, the other four are the next four entries. Nothing in the query says that — it falls out of sorting on the column the predicate names.

The baseline

Read it all, test it all

With no index, there is exactly one plan: a full table scan. Read every data page, and for every row on it, evaluate the predicate.

for each page p in STUDENT: for each row r on p: if 3.05 <= r.GPA <= 3.50: emit r

Twelve rows read, twelve comparisons, four pages. Five rows survive. The other seven were read and discarded, and the engine had no way to know in advance which seven they were.

Why it does not scale

The cost is the size of the table, not the size of the answer. Finding five students out of twelve is cheap; finding five out of five million means reading five million. The query is identical — only the table grew.

The structure we are standing on

The index over GPA

Same B+tree you met in the join lecture, now keyed on GPA: internal nodes hold separators and route a descent; every key lives at the leaf level in ascending order; the leaves are chained left to right.

The three motions · 1 of 3

Descend to the low end

Take the lower bound of the range — 3.05 — and descend the tree with it exactly as if you were looking up a single value.

The separators route you: 3.05 is not below 3.05, and it is below 3.45, so take the second child. You land in leaf 2.

The thing students get wrong here

You are not searching for a value. You are searching for a position — the first entry at or after 3.05. If 3.05 did not exist in the table at all, the descent would still succeed and land you on 3.20, the first entry past it. A range scan never needs its endpoints to be present.

Cost so far: 2 page reads — the root and one leaf. In a real tree of height 3 or 4 it would be 3 or 4, and the upper levels are almost always already in memory.

The three motions · 2 of 3

Walk forward along the leaves

From the landing position, read forward. Every entry you meet qualifies automatically — you are past the lower bound and have not yet reached the upper one. When a leaf runs out, follow its next-leaf pointer and keep going. The upper levels are never touched again.

Count what was avoided

Two leaf pages of four were read. Six index entries were examined, five of which qualified. Six of the twelve rows were never looked at in any form — the engine knows they cannot match without reading them, because sorted order guarantees it.

The three motions · 3 of 3

One comparison past the end

The walk has to stop somewhere, and the only way to know it has gone too far is to look at one entry that fails. Here that entry is 3.60: greater than 3.50, so the scan ends.

pos = descend( gpa_idx, 3.05 ) while pos.key <= 3.50: // the stop test emit row_at( pos ) pos = pos.next // along the leaf chain

That single wasted comparison is the entire overhead of knowing where the range ends. Compare it with the full scan, where every comparison that fails is wasted — seven of them here, and millions on a real table.

Why the upper bound needs no descent

A second descent to find 3.50 would cost another root-to-leaf trip and tell you nothing you will not learn for free by walking into it. You only ever descend for the start of the range.

Six comparisons produced five rows. A full scan needs twelve comparisons to produce the same five. The ratio only gets better as the table grows, because the index's cost follows the answer.

Look at the last two rows before you decide who won

On twelve rows the scan is not obviously worse — four sequential reads can beat three reads plus five random fetches. The index wins decisively as the table grows and the answer does not. That trade-off is what the next demonstrator is about.

The contrast that proves the point

Why a hash index cannot do this

A hash index answers GPA = 3.45 in one shot, often faster than a B+tree. Ask it for a range and it has nothing to offer.

Hashing deliberately destroys order. 3.05 and 3.20 are neighbors in value and land in unrelated buckets; there is no "next" to follow, and no way to know which buckets hold values in between without visiting them all.

The rule to remember

Hash indexes serve equality. B+trees serve equality and order: ranges, BETWEEN, < and >, prefix matches like LIKE 'Sm%', and ORDER BY without a sort. That breadth is why B+trees are the default index in essentially every relational engine, and hash indexes the specialist.

Where the real cost hides

The random-fetch tax

The walk gives you index entries. Unless the index holds everything the query asked for, each entry then costs a trip to the actual row.

In a clustered index the rows are stored in index order, so those trips are sequential and nearly free — the range is a contiguous stretch of the table too.

In a secondary index the rows sit wherever they were inserted. Index order tells you nothing about where they are, so each of the five matches is a separate random read, possibly a separate page.

The practical consequence

A secondary index on a range predicate is excellent when the answer is a handful of rows and poor when it is a third of the table — because the random fetches, not the walk, dominate. One clustered index per table; choose it for the column you range over most.

The way out of the tax

When the walk is the whole query

If every column the query needs is already in the index, there is no reason to visit the table at all. The walk returns the answer directly. This is an index-only scan, and the index is said to cover the query.

Our query wants StudentID, Name and GPA. An index on GPA alone carries GPA and a row pointer, so it must fetch. Widen it and the fetches disappear:

INDEX student_gpa_cover ON STUDENT(GPA, StudentID, Name)

Now the five matches come out of three page reads, full stop. No random fetches, clustered or not.

What it costs you

  • A wider index means fewer entries per page, so a taller tree and more pages to walk.
  • Every one of those columns must be maintained on every insert, update and delete.
  • It helps this query. Add a fourth column to the SELECT and the covering property is gone.

Judgment, not reflex

Cover a query when it is frequent, selective enough that the walk is short, and stable enough that the column list will not drift. Covering every query is how you end up with a table whose indexes are larger than its data and whose writes have slowed to a crawl.

The failure mode you will actually hit

What silently disables the index

The descent works by comparing the predicate's bound against stored keys. Anything that stops the engine comparing a bare column against a constant takes the descent away, and you are back to a full scan — with no error and no warning.

The index on GPA, or on Name, exists in every row of this table.
Written asWhat happensWrite it as
WHERE ROUND(GPA) = 3 the index stores GPA, not ROUND(GPA) — nothing to descend with WHERE GPA >= 2.5 AND GPA < 3.5
WHERE GPA + 0.5 > 4.0 same problem: the column is inside an expression WHERE GPA > 3.5
WHERE Name LIKE '%smith' a leading wildcard gives no prefix, so no start position exists WHERE Name LIKE 'smith%'
WHERE GPA = '3.45' a type mismatch may force a conversion on the column side WHERE GPA = 3.45

The test to apply

Ask: could I find the first matching entry by descending the tree with a value I already have? If the column is wrapped in anything, the answer is no. A predicate that passes this test is called sargable — searchable by argument. Some engines recognize and rewrite simple cases, but never rely on that.

Why the crossover is lower than you expect

Random reads cost far more than sequential ones, so k random fetches can exceed P sequential reads well before k approaches N. On rotating disks the rule of thumb was a few percent; on SSDs it is higher but the shape is the same. This is why the optimizer needs statistics — without an estimate of k it cannot choose, and a stale histogram is a classic cause of a suddenly slow query.

Wrap-up

Recap & what's next

Today

  • A range predicate picks out a contiguous stretch of the leaf level, because the leaves are sorted on that column.
  • Answering it is three motions: descend to the lower bound, walk forward along the leaf chain, stop one comparison past the upper bound.
  • You descend for the start only. The end is discovered for the price of a single failed comparison.
  • Cost follows the size of the answer, not the table — until the row fetches take over, at which point the scan wins.
  • Hash indexes cannot do any of this: they trade order away for equality speed.
  • Wrapping the column in a function removes the descent and silently restores the full scan.

Check yourself

  1. The lower bound 3.05 is deleted from the table. Does the range scan still work, and what does the descent land on?
  2. Why is there no second descent for the upper bound?
  3. WHERE GPA >= 3.45 returns half the table. The index still reads only two leaf pages — so why might the optimizer refuse to use it?
  4. You add Major to the SELECT list of a query served by a covering index. What changes in the plan?
  5. Give a predicate on Name that an index on Name can serve, and one it cannot.
