From diagram to schema

ER/EER → relational mapping
From the diagram to the tables

The payoff of conceptual design: a diagram you reasoned about carefully becomes a relational schema by a fixed procedure.

Seven steps take every ER construct to a relation; two more handle the EER extensions. No creativity required at this stage — the thinking already happened in the diagram.

Where we are

Recap & today's roadmap

So far

Unit 1 gave us ER and EER diagrams. Monday gave us the relational model: relations, keys, and the integrity constraints (entity, referential) a schema must respect.

Today we connect the two — the mechanical bridge that turns a diagram into a set of relation schemas. This closes Unit 2.

By the end of this lecture you can

  • Apply the 7-step algorithm to any ER diagram.
  • Decide where a foreign key goes for 1:1, 1:N, and M:N.
  • Map weak entities, multivalued attributes, and n-ary relationships.
  • Choose among the EER options for specialization.
  • Read a completed schema back against its diagram.

Motivation

Why a mechanical mapping?

Back in ER I we promised: get the diagram right and the tables follow. This is where we make good on it.

  • The mapping is deterministic. Given the same diagram, everyone lands on essentially the same schema — the design choices were already spent on the diagram.
  • It preserves the constraints we drew. Cardinality becomes foreign-key placement; participation becomes a NOT NULL decision.
  • It is the same routine a CASE tool runs when it generates DDL. Doing it by hand once means you can read and trust what a tool emits.

The pipeline, one step further

Requirements → ER/EER diagram → relational schema (today) → SQL CREATE TABLE (Unit 4). The schema you produce here is one short syntactic hop from running code.

Overview

The seven steps at a glance

  1. Regular (strong) entities → a relation with all simple attributes; pick a primary key.
  2. Weak entities → a relation that borrows the owner's key as part of its own.
  3. Binary 1:1 → a foreign key on one side.
  4. Binary 1:N → a foreign key on the "many" side.
  5. Binary M:N → a new relationship relation with two foreign keys.
  6. Multivalued attributes → a new relation, one row per value.
  7. N-ary relationships → a new relation with a foreign key per participant.

Then the EER extensions

Two more steps handle specialization/generalization and union types (categories). We take those after the core seven.

Step 1 of 7

Regular entities become relations

For each strong entity type, create a relation that includes all of its simple attributes. For a composite attribute, include only its leaf components. Choose one candidate key as the primary key.

Multivalued attributes are held back for Step 6; relationships are handled by Steps 3–5. This step just lays down the entity relations.

Our mini-world

STUDENT, COURSE, EMPLOYEE, and DEPARTMENT each become a relation whose primary key is the attribute we underlined in the diagram.

STUDENT(StudentID, Fname, Lname, Major)

COURSE(CourseID, Title, Credits)

EMPLOYEE(Ssn, Fname, Lname, Salary)

DEPARTMENT(Dnumber, Dname)

Underline marks the primary key. No foreign keys yet — relationships come next.

Step 2 of 7

Weak entities borrow a key

Create a relation for the weak entity with all its attributes, then add the owner's primary key as a foreign key. The primary key of the new relation is the owner's key plus the weak entity's partial key.

That composite key is exactly the "owner key + partial key = full identity" we drew as a dashed underline in ER II.

Constraints to carry over

The foreign key is NOT NULL (total participation), and it typically cascades on delete — a dependent cannot outlive its employee.

EMPLOYEE(Ssn, Fname, Lname, Salary)

DEPENDENT(Essn, Name, BirthDate, Relationship)

Essn → EMPLOYEE.Ssn is the borrowed key. The primary key is the pair (Essn, Name) — Name alone only distinguishes dependents within one employee.

Step 3 of 7

Binary 1:1 — a foreign key on one side

Pick one of the two relations and add the other's primary key as a foreign key. You can add the relationship's own attributes to the same relation.

Which side? Choose the side with total participation, so the foreign key is never NULL. If a department must have a manager but an employee need not manage, put the key in DEPARTMENT.

Alternatives

If both sides are total, the two entities can be merged into one relation. A cross-reference relation is a third option, rarely needed for 1:1.

DEPARTMENT(Dnumber, Dname, MgrSsn, MgrStart)

EMPLOYEE(Ssn, Fname, Lname, Salary)

MgrSsn → EMPLOYEE.Ssn carries the Manages relationship; MgrStart is its relationship attribute.

Step 4 of 7

Binary 1:N — the workhorse

Put the foreign key on the "many" side, referencing the primary key of the "one" side. Any relationship attributes go there too.

This is the most common case and it needs no new relation — the 1:N relationship dissolves into a single column.

Read it back

A department has many employees; each employee works for one department. So each EMPLOYEE row names its one department — the foreign key lives with the employee.

DEPARTMENT(Dnumber, Dname)

EMPLOYEE(Ssn, Fname, Lname, Salary, Dno)

Dno → DEPARTMENT.Dnumber. Total participation of EMPLOYEE in Works_for makes Dno NOT NULL.

Step 5 of 7

Binary M:N — a new relation

A many-to-many relationship cannot hide in either entity. Create a new relationship relation whose attributes are the two participants' primary keys, both as foreign keys. Their combination is the primary key.

This is the only place a relationship attribute — a grade, an hours count — can live.

The place grades belong

Grade is a property of the enrollment, not of the student or the course. It sits in the relationship relation.

STUDENT(StudentID, Fname, Lname)

COURSE(CourseID, Title)

ENROLLMENT(StudentID, CourseID, Grade)

Both parts are foreign keys, and together they are the primary key. Grade is the relationship attribute.

Step 6 of 7

Multivalued attributes get their own relation

A multivalued attribute can't sit in a single column — a relation holds atomic values. Create a new relation with the owner's primary key (as a foreign key) plus the attribute. One row per value.

The primary key is the foreign key together with the value attribute, so each (owner, value) pair appears at most once.

The double oval, resolved

The double oval we drew in ER I always lands here: it becomes its own small relation.

EMPLOYEE(Ssn, Fname, Lname)

EMP_PHONE(Essn, Phone)

Essn → EMPLOYEE.Ssn. An employee with three phone numbers has three rows here.

Step 7 of 7

N-ary relationships

For a relationship of degree three or more, create a new relation with a foreign key to each participating entity, plus any relationship attributes. The primary key is usually the combination of all the foreign keys — narrowed if a cardinality constraint allows.

It generalizes Step 5: an M:N is just the binary case of this rule.

Don't fake it

If three entities genuinely meet in one fact, model the single ternary relation — three separate binary relations may not reconstruct the same information.

SUPPLIER(Sid, Sname)

PART(Pid, Pname)

PROJECT(Jid, Jname)

SUPPLY(Sid, Pid, Jid, Qty)

One row records: supplier S supplied part P to project J, in quantity Qty.

EER extension · 1

Mapping specialization / generalization

A superclass with subclasses has four standard realizations. Take EMPLOYEE specialized into STAFF (has Grade) and FACULTY (has Rank). Pick an option to see the resulting relations and when it fits.

Option A — superclass plus subclass relations. Works for any specialization. Subclass relations share the superclass key. Reading a full faculty record needs a join.

EMPLOYEE(Ssn, Fname, Lname)

STAFF(Ssn, Grade)

FACULTY(Ssn, Rank)

STAFF(Ssn, Fname, Lname, Grade)

FACULTY(Ssn, Fname, Lname, Rank)

No EMPLOYEE relation — valid only if the specialization is total.

EMPLOYEE(Ssn, Fname, Lname, EmpType, Grade, Rank)

EmpType ∈ {staff, faculty}. Grade is NULL for faculty rows; Rank is NULL for staff rows.

EMPLOYEE(Ssn, Fname, Lname, IsStaff, IsFaculty, Grade, Rank)

Two boolean flags let one row be both staff and faculty at once.

EER extension · 2

Union types (categories)

A category is a subclass of the union of several superclasses — an OWNER might be a PERSON, a BANK, or a COMPANY. The superclasses have different keys, so there's nothing shared to borrow.

The fix is a surrogate key: give the category relation a new system-generated identifier, and carry that surrogate into each superclass relation as a foreign key.

Why a surrogate

Because Ssn, BankCode, and CompanyID aren't interchangeable, no natural attribute can serve as the category's key.

OWNER(OwnerID)

PERSON(Ssn, Name, OwnerID)

BANK(BankCode, Bname, OwnerID)

COMPANY(CompanyID, Cname, OwnerID)

OwnerID is the surrogate; each superclass points back to it.
