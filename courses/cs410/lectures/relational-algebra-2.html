Motivation

From product to join

A join is a Cartesian product with the nonsense pairings filtered out — bundled into one operation.

EMPLOYEE ⋈E.Dno = D.Dnumber DEPARTMENT  ≡  σE.Dno = D.Dnumber(EMPLOYEE × DEPARTMENT)

Both compute the same relation. Writing it as a join says what we mean — "line each employee up with their department" — and lets the system pick an efficient way to compute it rather than literally building the full product.

Three flavors ahead

The condition can be any comparison (theta join), just equalities (equijoin), or implied by shared attribute names (natural join).

Joins · 1

Theta join (θ)

The general form. The join condition θ is any boolean test that compares attributes across the two relations — not only equality.

R ⋈θ S

Every combined tuple that satisfies θ is kept; all columns of both relations appear in the result.

When it earns its keep

Ranges and inequalities: pair each employee with every pay GRADE whose LowSalary ≤ Salary ≤ HighSalary.

EMPLOYEE ⋈Salary ≥ MinPay GRADE

Because the condition is a ≥ comparison, this is a theta join that is not an equijoin. The result still holds every column of EMPLOYEE and every column of GRADE.

Joins · 2

Equijoin

An equijoin is a theta join whose condition uses only equality (=). It's the everyday case — matching a foreign key to the key it references.

EMPLOYEE ⋈Dno = Dnumber DEPARTMENT

The redundant column

An equijoin keeps both matched columns, so Dno and Dnumber both appear, holding identical values. Natural join fixes exactly that.

Each result row carries the full employee and the full department, with two equal department-number columns side by side — harmless, but redundant. That redundancy motivates the next slide.

Joins · 3

Natural join (∗)

The natural join assumes the join condition: equate every pair of attributes that share a name across the two relations, and keep just one copy of each.

EMPLOYEE ∗ DEPARTMENT

Here both relations name the department Dnumber, so the natural join matches on it and returns a single Dnumber column. It's the most common join in practice.

Names must line up

If the columns were Dno and Dnumber, you'd RENAME one first — otherwise there's nothing shared to match, and you'd get a full product.

The "for all" operation

Division (÷)

Division answers "for all" questions. Given R(A, B) and S(B), R ÷ S returns the A values that are paired with every B in S.

TAKES ÷ CS_CORE

"Which students have taken all of the CS core courses?" Only a student whose rows cover every required course qualifies.

The hard one

Division has no direct SQL keyword — you'll rebuild it from other operations. Recognizing "for all" phrasing is the trigger.

Summarizing

Aggregation & grouping (ℱ)

Aggregation collapses many tuples into summary values with functions like COUNT, SUM, AVG, MIN, MAX.

grouping attrs ℱ function list(R)

Attributes on the left of ℱ define the groups; the functions on the right are computed within each group. With no grouping attributes, the whole relation is one group.

Read it

DnoℱCOUNT(Ssn), AVG(Salary)(EMPLOYEE) — "per department, how many employees and what average salary."

DnoℱCOUNT, AVG(Salary)(EMPLOYEE)
DnoCountAvgSalary

One row per group. Grouping attribute Dno carries through; the aggregates summarize each group.

Keeping unmatched rows

Outer joins, in notation

An inner join silently drops rows with no match. When those rows matter — a department with no employees, an employee not yet assigned — an outer join keeps them and pads the missing side with NULL.

  • R S — left: all of R.
  • R S — right: all of S.
  • R S — full: all of both.

Why it's not optional

"List every department and its head count, including the empty ones" is impossible with an inner join — the zero-employee departments would vanish. The outer join is the only way to keep them.

Reading a whole query

A query as a tree

Any RA expression is a tree: relations at the leaves, operators at the internal nodes. Evaluation flows up from the leaves; each node hands its result relation to its parent.

πFname( σDname='Research'(EMPLOYEE ∗ DEPARTMENT) )

Why draw it

The tree is what the optimizer rearranges in Unit 7 — pushing a selection below a join shrinks the intermediate result without changing the answer.

Leaves are relations; internal nodes are operators; results flow up to the root.

The declarative counterpart

Relational calculus, briefly

Relational algebra says how to compute an answer. Relational calculus says only what the answer is, and lets the system find a way.

{ t.Fname | EMPLOYEE(t) ∧ t.Salary > 40000 }

Read: "the Fname of every tuple t in EMPLOYEE whose salary exceeds 40,000." No operation order is specified.

They're equivalent

Codd's theorem: relational algebra and (safe) relational calculus express exactly the same queries. Neither can ask something the other can't.

Why it matters for SQL

SQL is closer to calculus — you describe the result and the engine plans the algebra. Both mental models pay off next unit.

Putting it together

Worked example

Question. "Titles of the courses in which student S1 earned an A." Three relations are involved: STUDENT, ENROLLMENT, COURSE.

Build it step by step — filter first, join to reach the title, then project:

GOOD ← σStudentID='S1' ∧ Grade='A'(ENROLLMENT)
WITH ← GOOD ∗ COURSE
RESULT ← πTitle(WITH)

Trace it

  1. SELECT narrows ENROLLMENT to S1's A grades.
  2. Natural join on CourseID brings in each matching COURSE row.
  3. PROJECT keeps only the titles.

Selecting before joining is deliberate: the join then works on a handful of rows, not the whole enrollment table.
