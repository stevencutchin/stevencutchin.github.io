Relationships, cardinality, and weak entities

Entities on their own are a list of nouns. The design lives in how they connect.

We add the diamond to our vocabulary, then learn to constrain it: how many entities take part, whether they must, and what happens when an entity can't stand on its own.

Motivation

From things to connections

A database of unconnected entities can't answer the questions people actually ask.

STUDENT and COURSE as separate boxes can list students and list courses — but not "which courses is this student taking?" That question lives in the relationship between them.

The move

Almost every interesting query crosses a relationship. Modelling them well now is what makes the relational schema — and the joins you'll write in SQL — fall out cleanly later.

Building block 4

Relationships: the diamond

A relationship associates two or more entities. Like entities, it has a type (the template) and a set (the actual associations that hold right now).

In Chen notation it is a diamond, joined by lines to the entities it connects.

The number of participating entity types is the relationship's degree. Binary (degree 2) is by far the most common; ternary relationships exist and need care.

Read it both ways

"A STUDENT enrolls in a COURSE" and "a COURSE has enrolled STUDENTs" are the same relationship seen from each side.

Relationship type: a diamond between entities.

Constraining the diamond · 1

Cardinality ratios

A cardinality ratio caps how many entities on each side one entity can be linked to. Switch the ratio and watch the edge labels.

One-to-many: one A links to many B, but each B links to just one A. Example: a DEPARTMENT employs many EMPLOYEEs; each employee works for exactly one department.

Ratios in the wild · 1:1

One-to-one (1:1)

Each entity on one side pairs with at most one on the other. These are the rarest — often a sign the two entities could even be one.

Manages: a department has exactly one manager, and an employee manages at most one department.

Sanity check

If it's truly 1:1 and both sides are total, ask whether the attributes belong together in a single entity instead.

Manages: 1 on each side.

Ratios in the wild · 1:N

One-to-many (1:N)

The workhorse ratio. One entity on the "one" side owns many on the "many" side; each of those points back to a single owner.

Employs: a department has many employees, but each employee works for exactly one department.

Looking ahead

1:N maps to the relational model as a foreign key on the "many" side — no extra table needed. We'll see this in Unit 2.

Employs: 1 department to N employees.

Ratios in the wild · M:N

Many-to-many (M:N)

Both sides are open-ended. Each entity on either side can link to many on the other.

Enrolls_in: a student takes many courses, and a course enrolls many students.

Two things to remember

M:N always becomes its own table in the relational model — and it's the only place a relationship attribute (like a grade) can live. More on that shortly.

Enrolls_in: M students to N courses.

Constraining the diamond · 2

Participation: must you take part?

Cardinality caps how many. Participation says whether every entity must take part at all.

Total participation (a double line): every entity on that side must be in the relationship — every EMPLOYEE must work for some DEPARTMENT. Also called an existence dependency.

Partial participation (a single line): taking part is optional — not every employee manages a department.

Every employee must work for a department.

Constraining the diamond · 3

One label for both: (min, max)

Elmasri & Navathe often use an alternative that folds cardinality and participation into one pair on each edge. Read (min, max) as: each entity of this type takes part in at least min and at most max instances of the relationship.

  • min = 0 → partial participation (optional).
  • min ≥ 1 → total participation (required).
  • max carries the cardinality (1 or N).

Watch the side

The pair sits on the entity's own edge and describes that entity — the opposite convention from where you place 1/N. Pick one notation per diagram and stay consistent.

(1,1) on EMPLOYEE means exactly one — total. (1,N) on DEPARTMENT means one to many.

Refinements · 1

Attributes on a relationship

Some facts belong to the pairing, not to either entity. A Grade isn't a property of the student or of the course — it's a property of a particular enrollment.

Draw such an attribute as an oval hanging off the diamond.

The M:N test

On an M:N relationship the attribute must sit on the diamond — there's no single entity it could hide on. On a 1:N it can often move to the "many" side instead.

Grade is a property of the enrollment itself.

Refinements · 2

Recursive relationships & roles

An entity type can relate to itself. An employee supervises other employees — one EMPLOYEE type, playing two parts.

Because both ends are the same entity, we label each line with a role so the two sides are distinguishable: supervisor and supervisee.

This one is 1:N — one supervisor, many supervisees — and it's how you'd model an org chart or any hierarchy.

EMPLOYEE supervises EMPLOYEE — roles tell the ends apart.

Building block 5

Weak entities

A weak entity has no key of its own — it can't be identified without its owner. A DEPENDENT is only meaningful relative to the EMPLOYEE it belongs to, and two employees may each have a dependent named "Sam."

  • It borrows identity through an identifying relationship — a double diamond — to its owner.
  • Its own partial key (a dashed underline) distinguishes siblings under the same owner. Owner key + partial key = full identity.
  • It always has total participation (double line) and sits on the "many" side: no owner, no weak entity.
  • The weak entity itself is a double rectangle.

DEPENDENT is identified by its EMPLOYEE plus its own Name.

Judgement

Design pitfalls & tips

  • Resolve the ER I cliffhanger. When a would-be attribute needs to be shared or carry its own data — Department — promote it to an entity and connect it with a relationship. That's the call we deferred last lecture.
  • Put M:N attributes on the diamond. A grade, a role, an hours-worked count belongs to the pairing, not to either entity.
  • Don't fake a ternary with attributes, or split one into binaries carelessly. If three entities genuinely meet in one fact, model the ternary; check whether three binary relationships would lose information.
  • Name relationships as verbs and check the ratio by reading a real sentence in each direction. "One department, many employees" catches a flipped 1/N label instantly.
