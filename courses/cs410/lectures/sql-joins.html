SQL Joins
Inner, outer, left, and right

A join is the operation that turns a normalized schema back into the answers people actually asked for.

We will draw each join as an overlap diagram, then look underneath the picture at what the database really does — because the picture is a useful shorthand, not the definition.

Where we are

Recap & today's roadmap

You already met this

In relational algebra we defined the theta join, the equijoin, the natural join, and the outer joins. SQL is where you write them down for a real engine.

Every join in this lecture is an equijoin on a foreign key — by far the most common case, and the one the overlap diagrams describe honestly.

By the end of this lecture you can

  • Say what JOIN alone means, and why it is the inner join.
  • Choose between LEFT and RIGHT and explain which table is preserved.
  • Write the anti-join that finds unmatched rows.
  • Work around MySQL's missing FULL OUTER JOIN.
  • Say where the Venn picture stops being true.

Under the picture

What a join actually does

Conceptually the engine forms every pair of one row from the left and one from the right — the Cartesian product — then keeps the pairs for which the ON condition is true.

Four students × three departments = 12 candidate pairs. Three survive.

Real engines do not literally build all 12; that is what indexes and join algorithms are for, in Unit 7. But the meaning is defined this way, and every join type below is a rule about what to do with the pairs that fail.

NULL never matches

Dmitri's row fails against all three departments, because NULL = 10 is not false — it is unknown, which is not true, so the pair is dropped.

The 12 candidate pairs. Ticks are the pairs the ON condition accepts.

Vocabulary

Reading the overlap diagram

Each circle is one of the two tables. The three regions are the three fates a row can meet:

  • Left crescent — left rows that matched nothing. Kept only by a left or full join, padded with NULLs.
  • Overlap — pairs that satisfied ON. Kept by every join type except the anti-joins.
  • Right crescent — right rows that matched nothing. Kept only by a right or full join.

Shading a region means those rows appear in the result. The badge in each shaded region is how many rows it contributes for our data.

All three regions shaded — that is the full outer join, and the most rows any of these joins can produce here.

The default

JOIN and INNER JOIN are the same thing

Writing JOIN on its own gives you an inner join. INNER is optional noise; most style guides drop it.

Unmatched rows on either side are discarded silently. That silence is the most common source of quietly wrong reports: a student with no department simply vanishes from the count.

SELECT s.name, d.dname
FROM   student s
JOIN   department d ON s.dept_id = d.dept_id;

Ask yourself every time

If a row on either side has no partner, should it disappear from my answer? If the answer is no, you do not want an inner join.

Inner join: only the pairs that satisfy the ON condition. Dmitri has no department and Physics has no students, so neither appears. Three rows.

Preserve the left table

LEFT OUTER JOIN

LEFT JOIN keeps every row of the table named first, whether or not it found a partner. Unmatched rows get NULLs in all the right-hand columns.

OUTER is optional here too: LEFT JOIN and LEFT OUTER JOIN are identical.

SELECT s.name, d.dname
FROM   student s
LEFT JOIN department d ON s.dept_id = d.dept_id;

Read it as a promise

"Every student appears in my result, department or not." Left means the table on the left of the JOIN keyword — which is the one in the FROM clause.

Left outer join: every matched pair, plus every left row that matched nothing, padded with NULLs on the right. Dmitri appears with a NULL department. Four rows.

Preserve the right table

RIGHT OUTER JOIN

RIGHT JOIN is the mirror image: every row of the second table survives, and unmatched ones get NULLs on the left.

Note the result below has the same three matched rows as before, but Physics now appears with no student attached.

SELECT s.name, d.dname
FROM   student s
RIGHT JOIN department d ON s.dept_id = d.dept_id;

You will rarely write one

Any right join can be rewritten as a left join by swapping the two tables, and most teams standardise on left joins so that the preserved table is always the one you read first. Recognise RIGHT; prefer LEFT.

Right outer join: every matched pair, plus every right row that matched nothing, padded with NULLs on the left. Physics appears with a NULL student. Four rows.

Preserve both tables

FULL OUTER JOIN

Keeps everything: matched pairs, unmatched left rows, unmatched right rows. All three regions shaded.

MySQL does not implement it. You build it from a left join and a right join combined with UNION, which removes the duplicated matched rows.

-- Standard SQL; MySQL does not support it:
SELECT s.name, d.dname
FROM   student s
FULL OUTER JOIN department d ON s.dept_id = d.dept_id;

The MySQL idiom

SELECT s.name, d.dname
FROM   student s LEFT JOIN department d ON s.dept_id = d.dept_id
UNION
SELECT s.name, d.dname
FROM   student s RIGHT JOIN department d ON s.dept_id = d.dept_id;

Use UNION, not UNION ALL — the matched rows appear in both halves.

The crescents on their own

Anti-joins: finding what is missing

Shade only a crescent and you get the rows that failed to match. There is no ANTI JOIN keyword — write an outer join, then filter for the padding.

Students with no department.

SELECT s.name
FROM   student s
LEFT JOIN department d ON s.dept_id = d.dept_id
WHERE  d.dept_id IS NULL;

Departments with no students.

SELECT d.dname
FROM   student s
RIGHT JOIN department d ON s.dept_id = d.dept_id
WHERE  s.sid IS NULL;

Test a column that cannot be NULL on its own

Filter on the joined table's primary key (d.dept_id IS NULL), never on a nullable data column. A real NULL in that column would be indistinguishable from the padding.

The classic trap

ON versus WHERE on an outer join

On an inner join it makes no difference where you put a condition. On an outer join it changes the answer, and this is the single most common join bug.

Condition in WHERE

SELECT s.name, d.dname
FROM   student s
LEFT JOIN department d ON s.dept_id = d.dept_id
WHERE  d.dname = 'Computer Science';

The join runs, padding Dmitri with NULLs — then WHERE throws his row away, because NULL = 'Computer Science' is not true. You have silently turned it back into an inner join.

Condition in ON

SELECT s.name, d.dname
FROM   student s
LEFT JOIN department d
       ON s.dept_id = d.dept_id
      AND d.dname = 'Computer Science';

The condition is part of what counts as a match. Every student still appears; Cleo and Dmitri simply come back with a NULL department.

The rule

Conditions on the preserved table belong in WHERE. Conditions on the optional table belong in ON — unless you deliberately want to drop the unmatched rows.

Honesty about the model

Where the overlap picture breaks down

The diagrams are a good map of which rows survive on a foreign-key equijoin. They are not a definition of a join, and four things escape them:

  • Rows multiply. On an M:N join a left row matching three right rows produces three result rows. A join of a 100-row and a 100-row table can return 10,000 rows — a Venn diagram can never show a region larger than its circle.
  • The circles are not sets of the same things. STUDENT rows and DEPARTMENT rows have different schemas; nothing is literally "in both". The overlap is a set of pairs, not a set of shared members.
  • Only equijoins fit. ON s.gpa > d.min_gpa is a perfectly good join with no meaningful circles at all.
  • CROSS JOIN has no picture. There is no condition, so there is nothing to overlap.

The version that is always true

Pair every row with every row, keep the pairs that satisfy ON, then add back the unmatched rows from whichever sides the join type preserves, padded with NULL. Fall back to that sentence whenever the picture and the result disagree.

The rest of the family

CROSS, NATURAL, and USING

CROSS JOIN

SELECT s.name, d.dname
FROM   student s CROSS JOIN department d;

No condition, so every pair survives: 4 × 3 = 12 rows. Legitimate for generating combinations — and what you accidentally get if you list two tables in FROM and forget the WHERE.

USING

FROM student s JOIN department d
     USING (dept_id)

Shorthand when both columns have the same name. The joined column appears once in the result instead of twice.

NATURAL JOIN

SELECT * FROM student NATURAL JOIN department;

Joins on every pair of identically named columns, automatically.

Do not use it in your project

It is invisible: the join condition lives in your column names, so adding a created_at column to both tables silently changes what the query means. Write the ON clause out.

Self joins

Nothing special — join a table to itself with two aliases, as in the supervisor/supervisee relationship from ER II.

Wrap-up

Recap & what's next

Today

  • JOIN = INNER JOIN: matched pairs only.
  • LEFT / RIGHT name the table that is preserved, padded with NULL.
  • FULL OUTER keeps both sides; MySQL needs a UNION.
  • Outer join + IS NULL = anti-join, for finding what is missing.
  • ON and WHERE are not interchangeable on outer joins.

Check yourself

  1. Which join answers "list every department and how many students it has, including the empty ones"?
  2. Why does moving d.dname = 'Physics' from ON to WHERE change a left join's row count?
  3. Two tables of 100 rows each, joined M:N. What is the largest result the Venn picture would let you predict, and what is the real upper bound?
