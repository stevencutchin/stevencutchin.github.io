ER/EER → relational mapping

The diagram was never the destination. Now we turn it into tables — by a fixed procedure, not by taste.

Seven steps take any ER diagram to a relational schema; four more options handle EER. Get the diagram right and the tables fall out almost mechanically.

Motivation

Why the mapping is mechanical

The hard thinking happened in Unit 1. This step is deterministic: the same diagram always yields the same schema.

  • Each ER construct has a fixed relational counterpart — an entity becomes a relation, an M:N becomes a linking relation, and so on.
  • Because it's an algorithm, it's checkable: you can verify a schema against its diagram, and a tool can generate one from the other.
  • The judgment that remains is small and named — which side gets a 1:1 foreign key, which EER option to use — and we'll call out each choice as it arrives.

The 7-step algorithm

1Regular entity types

For each strong (regular) entity type, create a relation that includes all its simple attributes. For a composite attribute, include only its simple components (Name → Fname, Lname).

Choose one key attribute as the primary key. Multivalued and derived attributes are not included here — derived values aren't stored, and multivalued ones wait for Step 6.

On this diagram

Four strong entities → four relations. Foreign-key columns are still blank; relationships fill them in Steps 3–5.

STUDENT
StudentIDprimary key Fname Lname Major GPA
EMPLOYEE
Ssnprimary key Fname Lname
DEPARTMENT
Dnumberprimary key Dname

COURSE(CourseID, Title, Credits) maps the same way.

The 7-step algorithm

2Weak entity types

A weak entity has no key of its own, so its relation borrows the owner's primary key as a foreign key. The primary key of the weak relation is that foreign key plus the weak entity's partial key.

DEPENDENT is identified by its EMPLOYEE plus its own Name — so the relation keys on both. Two employees can each have a "Sam" and stay distinct.

Total participation shows up here

Because a dependent can't exist without its employee, the foreign key is NOT NULL and typically carries ON DELETE CASCADE.

DEPENDENT
EssnPK part · FK → EMPLOYEE Dep_namePK part · partial key Relationship Bdate

The 7-step algorithm

3Binary 1:1 relationships

The usual choice is the foreign key approach: take one of the two relations and add the other's primary key to it as a foreign key, along with any relationship attributes.

Which side? Put the foreign key in the relation with total participation — that keeps the column from filling with NULLs. Every DEPARTMENT has a manager, but most employees manage nothing, so Mgr_ssn lives in DEPARTMENT.

Two other options

If both sides are total, consider merging the two entities into one relation. A separate relationship relation is also legal but rarely worth it for 1:1.

DEPARTMENT  — after Manages
Dnumberprimary key Dname Mgr_ssnFK → EMPLOYEE Mgr_startrel. attribute

Read the NULL test aloud

Put Mgr_ssn in DEPARTMENT and every row has a value. Put a Manages FK in EMPLOYEE instead and nearly every row is NULL — same data, worse schema.

The 7-step algorithm

4Binary 1:N relationships

No choice to make here: put the primary key of the "1" side into the relation on the "N" side as a foreign key. Any relationship attributes go on the N side too.

Each employee works for exactly one department, so DEPARTMENT's key becomes Dno in EMPLOYEE. No extra table — this is why 1:N is the cheapest relationship to store.

Recursive 1:N is the same move

Supervises relates EMPLOYEE to itself, so EMPLOYEE gets a Super_ssn foreign key pointing back at its own Ssn. It's nullable — the top of the hierarchy supervises but reports to no one.

EMPLOYEE  — after Works_for & Supervises
Ssnprimary key Fname Lname DnoFK → DEPARTMENT Super_ssnFK → EMPLOYEE

Two 1:N relationships, two foreign-key columns — both on the many side, which for Supervises is EMPLOYEE itself.

The 7-step algorithm

5Binary M:N relationships

A many-to-many relationship can't be a foreign key on either side — so it gets its own relation, called a relationship (or linking) relation.

Include the primary keys of both entities as foreign keys; together they form the primary key of the new relation. This is the only place a relationship attribute like Grade can live.

The M:N test, paid off

In ER II we said a grade belongs to the enrollment, not to the student or the course. Here's why that mattered: ENROLLMENT is the row that a grade finally has a home on.

ENROLLMENT
StudentIDPK part · FK → STUDENT CourseIDPK part · FK → COURSE Graderel. attribute

Primary key = the pair of foreign keys. One row per student-in-a-course; Grade rides along.

The 7-step algorithm

6Multivalued attributes

A relation stores one value per cell, so a multivalued attribute needs its own relation. It holds the value plus a foreign key to the owner; the primary key is the two together.

DEPARTMENT's Locations become DEPT_LOCATIONS — one row per (department, location) pair. A department in three buildings is three rows.

Composite & multivalued

If the multivalued attribute is also composite, include each simple component as its own column in the new relation.

DEPT_LOCATIONS
DnumberPK part · FK → DEPARTMENT LocationPK part

The 7-step algorithm

7n-ary relationships

For a relationship of degree three or more, create a relationship relation holding a foreign key to each participating entity, plus any relationship attributes.

Suppose an INSTRUCTOR teaches a COURSE in a given TERM. That single fact ties three entities together, so TEACHES carries three foreign keys. The primary key is normally all of them together.

Don't fake it

Three binary relationships are not the same as one ternary — they can't record which instructor taught which course in which term as one linked fact. If the fact is genuinely three-way, model it that way.

TEACHES
Instr_idPK part · FK → INSTRUCTOR CourseIDPK part · FK → COURSE Term_idPK part · FK → TERM Roomrel. attribute

A degree-3 relationship → one relation, three foreign keys. A cardinality constraint on one side can shrink the primary key.

Mapping EER · 1 of 3

Specialization: multiple relations

Say STUDENT specializes into GRAD and UNDERGRAD. There are four standard ways to map it; the first two use several relations.

8A · superclass + subclass relations

A relation for STUDENT with the shared attributes, then one per subclass keyed on the same primary key (also a foreign key to STUDENT), holding only that subclass's attributes. Always works — any specialization, disjoint or overlapping, total or partial.

8B · subclass relations only

One relation per subclass, each carrying the shared attributes and its own. No STUDENT relation. Only safe when the specialization is total (no plain students) and disjoint (no one in two subclasses) — otherwise you lose or duplicate rows.

8A → STUDENT, then GRAD_STUDENT and UNDERGRAD
StudentIDPK of all three Name, Majorin STUDENT Advisor, Thesisin GRAD_STUDENT Class_yearin UNDERGRAD

Mapping EER · 2 of 3

Specialization: a single relation

The other two options fold everything into one relation and use an extra column to record type. They trade joins for NULLs.

8C · one type attribute

A single relation with all shared and all subclass attributes, plus one type column naming the subclass. For disjoint specializations. Attributes of the other subclass sit NULL in each row.

8D · several boolean flags

One relation with a boolean flag per subclass (Is_grad, Is_undergrad). Needed when subclasses overlap — an entity can be in more than one — since a single type column can't say "both."

8C · STUDENT (disjoint)
StudentIDprimary key Name S_typegrad / undergrad ThesisNULL if undergrad Class_yearNULL if grad

The trade-off, stated plainly

Single-relation options avoid joins but invite NULLs; multiple-relation options avoid NULLs but need joins. Pick by how the data is queried.

Mapping EER · 3 of 3

Union types need a surrogate key

A union type (category) has superclasses with different keys. Imagine a SPONSOR that may be a STUDENT (keyed by StudentID), an EMPLOYEE (keyed by Ssn), or an outside ORG (keyed by Tax_id).

There's no shared key to borrow, so create a new relation for the category and give it a surrogate key — a system-generated identifier. Each superclass relation carries that surrogate as a foreign key to tie back.

Why not reuse a key?

StudentID, Ssn, and Tax_id have different domains and formats. A surrogate is the one identifier all category members can share.

SPONSOR (category)
Sponsor_idsurrogate primary key Member_typestudent / employee / org
STUDENT  (one of the superclasses)
StudentIDprimary key Sponsor_idFK → SPONSOR

Judgment

Where judgment actually enters

  • 1:1 side. Put the foreign key on the total-participation side to dodge NULLs. When both are total, weigh merging the two relations into one.
  • Choosing an EER option. 8B needs total and disjoint; 8C needs disjoint; 8D covers overlapping. Reach for 8A when unsure — it always works.
  • NULLs vs. joins. The single-relation EER options and the M:N-vs-1:N call are all really the same trade-off. Decide from the expected workload, not habit.
  • Keep the audit trail. Every foreign key should point back to one relationship or one attribute in the diagram. A foreign key you can't explain is a mapping error.

Sanity check the whole schema

Count relations against the diagram: one per strong entity, one per weak entity, one per M:N and n-ary relationship, one per multivalued attribute. Anything left over — or missing — is a bug.
