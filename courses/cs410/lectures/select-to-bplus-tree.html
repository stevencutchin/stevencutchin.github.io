Query processing · the translation nobody shows you

SQL says what.
The engine writes the how.

You write one declarative sentence. What runs is a small program: a handful of B+tree operations, in a chosen order, pulling rows through a chain of operators until the output table is full.

Nothing in your SELECT says descend a tree, scan a leaf chain, or stop at a key. Yet that is all the machine can do. Today we follow one query with a WHERE and a JOIN all the way down to those operations, and back up to the rows that come out.

One statement in. Two trees, four operators, and a fixed order out.

Motivation

Why this is worth an hour

Every performance conversation you will ever have about a database is a conversation about this translation.

  • EXPLAIN becomes readable. The tree it prints is exactly the physical plan we build on slide 12, with the same operator names and the same bottom-up data flow.
  • You learn which predicates are free and which are not. A WHERE clause that becomes a tree seek costs almost nothing. The same clause written slightly differently reads every row in the table. The difference is a rule you can apply by eye.
  • Index choice stops being superstition. You will see precisely which plan step an index serves, and therefore which index is worth its maintenance cost.
  • It is the bridge to the rest of the unit. Cost estimation and optimization only make sense once you can see the alternatives being costed.

The claim we are going to defend

A SELECT with a WHERE and a JOIN compiles into a plan whose every leaf is one of just five B+tree operations: descend, position, scan next, stop at key, fetch by pointer. The operators above the leaves add nothing the tree cannot do — they only decide which of those five happens next.

Where this sits

The join deck established how one join mechanism works on leaf chains. This deck is the layer above: how a query gets turned into a sequence of such mechanisms in the first place. Cost estimation and the optimizer's search over plans come next.

The running example

One query, two relations,
two indexes

Our UNIVERSITY mini-world. Name the students in CS 410 who are juniors or above, with the grade they earned.

STUDENT(StudentID, Name, Year, Major) ENROLLED_IN(StudentID → STUDENT, CourseID → COURSE, Grade)

SELECT s.Name, e.Grade FROM STUDENT s JOIN ENROLLED_IN e ON s.StudentID = e.StudentID WHERE e.CourseID = 'CS410' AND s.Year >= 3;

Three facts that decide everything

There is an index on ENROLLED_IN(CourseID) — so the WHERE on CourseID can become a seek. STUDENT's index is on its primary key, which is also the join column — so the join can become a probe. And there is no index on Year — so that predicate has nowhere to go but a per-row test.

The specification

What must come out

Read declaratively, the statement means: form every pairing of a student with an enrollment, throw away the pairings that fail the three conditions, keep two columns of what is left.

πName, Grade( σCourseID='CS410' ∧ Year≥3 ∧ s.StudentID=e.StudentID( STUDENT × ENROLLED_IN ))

Five students × six enrollments = 30 candidate pairings, of which 2 survive. That is the answer, and any plan that produces anything else is wrong. Everything that follows is about reaching those two rows without ever building the 30.

Why the definition is not a plan

At real scale this reading is unrunnable. Eight thousand students against fifty thousand enrollments is 400 million pairings materialized and then discarded, to return a few dozen rows. The relational algebra defines the answer, not the route.

Five stages, text to rows

Stages 1–3

Mechanical. Same result every time for the same text, regardless of what is in the tables.

Stage 1 of 5 · syntax

Parse: three clauses,
three jobs

The parser does not understand your query. It splits it into clauses, resolves every name against the catalog, and hands on a tree. Three clauses, three distinct roles:

What each clause contributes to the plan.
ClauseBecomes
FROM the set of inputs, and therefore the leaves of the plan tree
WHERE + ON a bag of conjuncts — one condition each, joined by AND, to be placed wherever they can be tested
SELECT the output columns — the projection at the top

The one idea that matters here

ON and WHERE are pooled. For an inner join the engine does not care which clause a condition arrived in; it collects all three conditions into one bag and decides independently where each one is best evaluated. (For outer joins the distinction is real and changes the answer — a later deck.)

Stage 2 of 5 · logical algebra

The canonical plan:
correct, and unusable

The parse tree translates into relational algebra by a fixed recipe: cross-product everything in FROM, apply the whole condition bag as one selection, project at the end.

πName, Grade( σall three conjuncts( STUDENT × ENROLLED_IN ) )

This is a faithful statement of the answer, and it is the plan no engine would ever run. Its defect is structural, not cosmetic: the × is at the bottom, so the largest intermediate relation in the whole query is built first, and every condition that could have prevented that work is tested afterwards.

What it would cost here

30 intermediate rows for a 2-row answer. At table scale, 400 million. The cross product is also the one operator whose output size you cannot reduce by being clever about access paths — it is |R| × |S| by definition.

Why bother writing it down

Because it is the definition of correctness against which every rewrite is checked. A rewrite is legal exactly when it produces the same relation as this tree. That is the whole discipline of the next slide.

Stage 3 of 5 · heuristic rewriting

Push the conditions down

Two transformations, applied in this order, turn the strawman into something runnable. Both are provably answer-preserving.

  1. Push each single-table selection to its table. CourseID='CS410' mentions only ENROLLED_IN, so it can be applied before the pairing rather than after. Same for Year≥3 on STUDENT. Filtering early shrinks everything downstream.
  2. Fuse the remaining condition into the product. A σ over a × whose condition compares the two inputs is a join: σp(R × S) ≡ R ⋈p S. This is a rewrite of notation, but it is the one that matters — it turns a build-then-discard into a single operator that can be implemented cleverly.
πName, Grade( σYear≥3(STUDENT) ⋈s.StudentID = e.StudentID σCourseID='CS410'(ENROLLED_IN) )

Still no decisions made

Nothing here says how to evaluate the join or the selections. This is still a logical plan — it has shape, but no algorithms. The ⋈ is a promise, not a procedure.

Stage 4 of 5 · the slide to remember

Every condition becomes
one of three things

This is where a WHERE clause turns into B+tree work — or fails to. The deciding question for each conjunct: can this be answered by positioning in a tree, or only by looking at a row?

The three conjuncts of our query, and the fate of each.
ConjunctShape BecomesRows it saves
e.CourseID = 'CS410' indexed column = constant A seek into ENROLLED_IN(CourseID) at key CS410, plus a stop condition when the key changes. Called sargable. 3 of 6 entries never read at all
s.StudentID = e.StudentID column = column, two tables The join's matching rule. Because STUDENT is indexed on this column, it also becomes a probe key — one descent per row from the other side. all 30 pairings avoided
s.Year >= 3 unindexed column vs constant A residual filter — a test applied to each STUDENT row after it has been fetched. Nothing to position on. none; the work is already spent

The rule in one line

A condition can become a seek when it compares an indexed column, standing bare on one side, to something constant for the query. Then the predicate is a position in a sorted sequence, and the tree can jump there.

How to destroy a usable index by accident

Wrap the column in anything and the seek is gone, even with the index in place: WHERE YEAR(EnrollDate) = 2026, WHERE UPPER(Name) = 'RUIZ', WHERE Year + 1 >= 4. The tree is sorted by the column, not by a function of it, so there is no position to jump to and every row must be read and tested.

Stage 4 of 5 · the instruction set

The whole instruction set:
five operations

Whatever the plan looks like, this is all it can ask a B+tree to do. Every access method in every engine is a composition of these.

The B+tree operations, their cost, and which part of a query produces them.

Where the bill comes from

Only descend and fetch are expensive, and both are random reads. A good plan is one that does few descents and few fetches, and gets as much as it can out of scan_next, which is nearly free.

The fetch you can delete

If the index's leaf entries already carry every column the query needs, fetch never happens — the index covers the query. Adding StudentID and Grade to our CourseID index would remove three page reads from the plan we are about to run.

Stage 4 of 5 · the decisions

One access path per table

With the conjuncts classified, each leaf of the logical plan gets an access method. Two tables, two decisions — and the second decision depends on the first.

The two choices, and the reasoning behind each.
TableChosen pathBecause
ENROLLED_IN Index seek on (CourseID) at CS410, then fetch per entry a sargable predicate on an indexed column, and it is selective — 3 of 6 rows here, 120 of 50,000 at scale
STUDENT Index probe on the clustered primary key, driven by each enrollment's StudentID the join column is the key of a clustered index, so a probe lands directly on the row — no fetch at all

Why STUDENT is the inner side, not the outer

The filtered ENROLLED_IN side yields only 3 rows, so using it to drive 3 probes is cheap. Driving the other way — scan all students, probe enrollments — would cost 5 probes here and 8,000 at scale. The selective side goes outer.

Stage 5 of 5 · execution

Rows are pulled, not pushed

Every operator in the tree exposes the same three calls, and that is the entire execution engine:

open() // get ready; for a seek, descend the tree next() // return one row, or "done" close() // release the cursor

The client calls next() on the top operator. PROJECT calls next() on FILTER, which calls next() on the JOIN, which calls next() on the seek, which performs one scan_next on a leaf. A row then travels back up, is probed against, tested, trimmed, and handed out.

Two consequences worth having

Results start before the work finishes. Our first output row is ready after 5 page reads of the eventual 11 — which is why LIMIT 1 on this plan is genuinely cheap. And no intermediate relation is ever stored. The 30-row cross product of the canonical plan never exists anywhere.

Which operators break the stream

Seek, probe, filter and project are pipelined — one row in, one row out. Sorting, grouping, and the build side of a hash join are blocking: they must consume their whole input before emitting anything. Add an ORDER BY on an unindexed column and the early-rows property on the left disappears.

Count the work

11 page reads for a 2-row answer. Three of the six index entries were ever looked at, and three of the five students were ever read. The canonical plan from slide 7 would have built 30 intermediate rows to produce the same two.

Why this plan and not another

The same query at real scale

Six rows prove nothing about cost. Scale the tables up and keep the plan: 50,000 enrollments on 500 pages, 8,000 students on 200 pages, 120 students in CS 410, both trees of height 3 with the upper levels cached.

Three plans for the same query. Page reads, dominated by the random ones.
PlanHow it gets the enrollments How it gets the studentsPages
Chosen: seek + index nested loop seek to CS410, scan 120 entries, fetch 120 rows → ~124 120 probes, 1 leaf read each → 120 ~244
Scan + index nested loop full scan of the table → 500 120 probes → 120 ~620
Scan + hash join full scan → 500 full scan to build the hash table → 200 ~700

Now change one number

Suppose the WHERE matched 20,000 enrollments instead of 120. The chosen plan becomes 20,000 fetches plus 20,000 probes — 40,000 random reads, far worse than the hash join's 700 sequential ones. Same query text, same indexes, opposite decision. Selectivity, not the index's existence, decides.

And the plan we skipped

If both sides were indexed on StudentID, a merge join over the two leaf chains becomes available: no probes, one forward pass each, cost additive rather than per-row. That mechanism is the subject of the companion deck — and the optimizer costs it against these three every time.

Wrap-up

Recap & what's next

Today

  • A query becomes rows in five stages: parse, canonical algebra, rewrite, physical plan, execute. Only the last one reads a page.
  • WHERE and ON dissolve into a bag of conjuncts, and each conjunct is placed independently.
  • Each conjunct becomes exactly one of three things: a seek, a join matching rule, or a residual filter. The first is nearly free, the last is paid for per row.
  • Underneath, the plan has only five moves available: descend, position, scan next, stop at, fetch. Descend and fetch are the expensive ones.
  • Execution is pull-based: one row at a time, up through the operators, with no intermediate relation stored anywhere.
  • The plan is chosen on selectivity. An index is not "faster" — it is only faster for a predicate selective enough to justify the random reads.

Check yourself

  1. Which of the three conjuncts could have been eliminated by a different index, and what index would it be?
  2. At step 8 the plan discards a row after spending 3 page reads on it. Could any plan have avoided that cost? Why or why not?
  3. Rewrite the WHERE as e.CourseID LIKE 'CS41%'. Is it still sargable? What about LIKE '%410'?

  5. The query returns 2 rows and reads 11 pages. Give a query over the same tables that returns 2 rows and must read all 500.
