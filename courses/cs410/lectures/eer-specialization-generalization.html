EER Modeling
Specialization, generalization & inheritance

Plain ER treats every entity type as flat. The real world has kinds within kinds — and rules about them.

The enhanced ER model adds three things: a way to say "this kind of thing is a special case of that kind," a way to share attributes without duplicating them, and a vocabulary for the constraints that govern the split. Each one earns its place by solving a concrete modelling problem.

Motivation · why EER exists

When one entity type isn't enough

Suppose EMPLOYEE holds everyone — but salaried staff have a salary, hourly staff have a pay rate, and only engineers have a PE license number.

Plain ER leaves two bad options, and the whole point of EER is to escape them:

Option A — one bloated entity

Cram every attribute into EMPLOYEE. Now most rows have NULLs in the columns that don't apply, and nothing records which attributes go together.

Option B — separate entities

Split into ENGINEER, SECRETARY, MANAGER. Now Name, SSN, and Address are duplicated in each, and "they're all employees" is lost — every relationship to EMPLOYEE must be redrawn three times.

The EER move

Keep one EMPLOYEE with the shared attributes, and hang specialized subclasses off it for the parts that differ. Shared data lives once; specific data lives where it applies; no NULLs for inapplicable attributes. Every feature in this lecture is a refinement of that one idea.

Building block 1

Subclasses & superclasses

A subclass is a meaningful sub-grouping of a superclass: GRAD_STUDENT and UNDERGRAD_STUDENT are subclasses of STUDENT.

The relationship is IS-A: every grad student is a student. A subclass member is always also a member of the superclass — it's the same real-world entity, seen at a finer grain.

Not a normal relationship

IS-A isn't a diamond. It doesn't connect two different entities; it says one entity type is a role or type of another. That's why it gets its own notation.

The circle and subset symbols mark an IS-A hierarchy.

Building block 2 · the payoff

Attribute inheritance

A subclass inherits every attribute of its superclass, and participates in every relationship the superclass has.

So GRAD_STUDENT automatically has StudentID, Name, and Major — defined once on STUDENT — plus its own extras like Advisor and Research_area.

Why this is the whole point

Inheritance is what makes Option A's NULLs and Option B's duplication both go away. Shared structure is declared one time and flows down; each subclass only adds what's genuinely special about it.

Reading the model

A GRAD_STUDENT entity effectively carries:

  • Inherited: StudentID (key), Name, Major
  • Own: Advisor, Research_area

The key always comes from the superclass — subclass members are identified the same way as any member of the superclass.

Two directions · 1

Specialization — top-down

Specialization starts from a superclass and defines subclasses on it: "a STUDENT is either a GRAD_STUDENT or an UNDERGRAD_STUDENT."

Why you'd specialize:

  • A sub-grouping has attributes the others don't (Advisor for grads).
  • A sub-grouping takes part in relationships the others don't (only grads assist a COURSE as a TA).
  • A constraint applies to only part of the superclass.

No such difference? Don't specialize — a flat entity is simpler.

Advisor lives only on GRAD_STUDENT.

Two directions · 2

Generalization — bottom-up

Generalization is the same picture read the other way. You already have STUDENT and EMPLOYEE, notice they share Name, SSN, and Address, and factor those up into a new superclass PERSON.

Why you'd generalize: to capture commonality you discover after the fact — remove duplicated attributes, and let a single relationship (PERSON lives_at ADDRESS) stand in for what would otherwise be two.

Same diagram, opposite thinking

Specialization = top-down (split a whole). Generalization = bottom-up (unify the parts). The finished EER diagram looks identical; only the design process differs.

The arrows of thought point up; the notation is the same.

Constraint 1

Disjoint (d) vs. overlapping (o)

This constraint asks: can one entity belong to more than one subclass at the same time?

Disjoint (d) — no. A STUDENT is a GRAD_STUDENT or an UNDERGRAD_STUDENT, never both.

Overlapping (o) — yes. A PERSON might be both a STUDENT and an EMPLOYEE at once (a grad who also works as a TA).

Why declare it

It's a real business rule. Marked disjoint, the schema can forbid a person from being filed under two subclasses; marked overlapping, it must allow it. Guessing wrong here corrupts data later.

How to decide

Find one real entity that plausibly sits in two subclasses. If it can exist, you're overlapping. If it's nonsense, you're disjoint.

Watch for

"Type" words often signal disjoint (a flight is national or international). Roles often signal overlapping (a person's roles stack up).

Constraint 2

Total vs. partial

This constraint asks: must every superclass member belong to some subclass?

Total (a double line to the circle): every STUDENT must be a grad or an undergrad — the subclasses are exhaustive.

Partial (a single line): a superclass member may belong to none. Some EMPLOYEEs are neither SECRETARY, ENGINEER, nor MANAGER.

Why it's separate from disjoint

The two are independent — you can have any of the four combinations. "Exactly one" is disjoint + total; "at most one" is disjoint + partial; "any number" is overlapping; and so on.

Double line = total: no student outside the two subclasses.

Refinement

How is membership decided?

Predicate-defined (attribute-defined): a condition on an attribute decides the subclass. If STUDENT has a Level attribute, then Level = 'grad' puts a student in GRAD_STUDENT automatically. The defining predicate is written on the subclass line.

User-defined: no rule computes membership — someone decides case by case, and the DBMS just records it. Which employees are MANAGERs isn't derivable from a column.

Why the distinction earns its keep

If membership is predicate-defined, the model already tells you (and later the DBMS) how to place every entity — no separate bookkeeping. User-defined membership must be stored and maintained by hand, which is a cost worth seeing in the diagram.

Special case

When one attribute defines several subclasses at once (Level → grad/undergrad), that's an attribute-defined specialization; the attribute name is written next to the circle.

Multiple inheritance

When a subclass has two parents

If every subclass has just one superclass, the diagram is a hierarchy (a tree). Allow a subclass with two or more superclasses and it becomes a lattice.

A shared subclass inherits from all its parents. A TEACHING_ASSISTANT is a STUDENT and is an EMPLOYEE — it inherits StudentID and job attributes both. Membership requires being in both (an AND / intersection).

Why allow it

Real classifications cut along several dimensions at once. Forcing a tree would duplicate the TA's data under STUDENT and again under EMPLOYEE — exactly the redundancy EER exists to remove.

TA inherits from STUDENT and EMPLOYEE.

The subtle one

Union types — the "or" subclass

A category (union type) is a subclass whose members come from a union of superclasses. A library BORROWER is a STUDENT or an EMPLOYEE or a DEPARTMENT — any one of them can check out a book.

Marked with a circle containing ∪. Each member belongs to exactly one of the superclasses, and inherits only that one's attributes.

Category vs. shared subclass — the trap

A shared subclass is an AND (a TA is a student and an employee). A category is an OR (a borrower is a student or an employee or a department). Same circles, opposite meaning — read the ∪.

∪ in the circle: a borrower is any one of the three.

Judgement

When to specialize — and when not to

  • Specialize for a reason. Add a subclass only when it has its own attributes, its own relationships, or a constraint the others don't share. A subclass that adds nothing is just clutter — collapse it back.
  • Name the two constraints explicitly. Every specialization is disjoint-or-overlapping and total-or-partial. Leaving them unmarked hides a decision the relational schema will force anyway.
  • AND vs. OR. Two parents that must both hold → shared subclass. Membership from any one of several types → category. Don't mix them up.
  • Remember the payoff. The whole apparatus exists to avoid NULLs for inapplicable attributes and duplication of shared ones. If a design reintroduces either, reconsider it.
  • It still has to become tables. Next unit we map all of this to relations — and there's more than one way to map a specialization, each with tradeoffs.
