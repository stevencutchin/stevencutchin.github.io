A schema from live research

Why this ER diagram
looks like this

Three relations hold every claim about every artwork in the system — and none of them name an artist, a period, or a medium.

The OpenPipe asset schema is a deliberate departure from the modeling we have been practicing. This is the argument for the departure, built from the rules of the domain it serves.

Motivation

What OpenPipe is

An open-source content management and production pipeline for high-resolution digital art collections, built at Boise State with the School of Arts and community museum partners.

  • Captures panoramic and stereo imagery of artworks and sites, then stitches, tiles, and analyzes it on campus clusters and AWS.
  • Delivers any tracked asset by URI to a laptop, phone, desktop, or the CAVE.
  • Federates across external collections — the Cleveland Museum of Art, the Getty — alongside locally captured work.

Scale

One panoramic collection is about 1.5 GB. A single capture trip produced 300 GB; eleven trips total roughly 3.3 TB. A class of fifteen students would generate 4.5 TB. Medium scale — large enough that design mistakes are expensive to undo.

The question for today

Not how the pipeline works, but why the database underneath it is modeled the way it is — and why that model breaks the habits of Unit 1 on purpose.

The rule the schema serves · 1 of 4

The hardest case: identity itself

Consider cataloging a collection of Hindu devotional sculpture.

  • A devi is known by dozens of names. The thousand-name stotras treat that multiplicity as the subject, not as ambiguity awaiting resolution.
  • Whether Durga, Kali, Parvati, Uma, and Gauri are one goddess in many forms or distinct goddesses is answered differently by different traditions, regions, and texts — and each answer is correct within its own frame.
  • Avatar relations compound it. Vaishnava traditions disagree among themselves about whether Krishna is an avatar of Vishnu or the source of whom Vishnu is an expansion.

Why this is worse than missing data

There is no hidden fact that settles it. A Name column assumes a thing has a name; a primary key assumes a thing is one thing. Both assumptions fail here — not from lack of evidence, but because the question has no frame-independent answer.

A conventional schema is not merely lossy against this domain. It is category-mismatched: it cannot express the situation even in principle. And this is the normal condition of digital humanities work, not an exotic corner of it.

The rule the schema serves · 2 of 4

The everyday case: galleries disagree

The same structure appears in ordinary cataloging, in a milder form that every federated collection hits immediately.

  • Every gallery maintains its own tag set — its own vocabulary for period, school, medium, and form.
  • A work can genuinely belong to several schools or periods at once. Scholars disagree in good faith about the artist and the date.
  • A medium is wood, glass, steel, acrylic, oil, chalk — or several of them, described differently by each institution that holds the piece.

Two kinds of disagreement, one solution

Here the disagreement is epistemic: someone made the reliquary, and the institutions have different evidence. On the previous slide it was ontological: there is no fact to be right about. A schema that handles the second handles the first for free — so we design for the harder one.

The rule the schema serves · 3 of 4

What the conventional design costs

Model this the way we modeled STUDENT and it looks reasonable:

ARTWORK(ArtworkID, Title, Artist, Period, School, Medium, Date)

One column per property. One value per column. A student who has finished Unit 1 would draw exactly this.

Two failures, both fatal

  • It adjudicates on write. One Artist column forces someone to pick a winner at ingest. The dissenting attribution is destroyed — not flagged, not stored, gone.
  • It cannot hold the vocabulary. The Getty returns terms we do not control, a curator invents a field mid-collection, and a new partner museum arrives with its own scheme. Each one is a DDL change.

The first failure is the serious one. Adding columns is tedious; losing a scholarly claim is lossy against the domain, and no amount of column-adding repairs it.

The rule the schema serves · 4 of 4

Record claims, don't adjudicate

The design decision that drives everything else: the database stores what each institution says, and resolves nothing.

  • We do not surrender an external gallery's tag set. We keep it intact and maintain our own internal tag set alongside it, filled at ingest.
  • An asset carrying two conflicting attributions is not an error state. It is the correct record of a real disagreement.
  • Resolution, where anyone wants it, is a post-processing question for tools that sweep the data — never a constraint enforced at write time.

Reframing

Disagreement stops being a conflict to clean up and becomes data — and, as we will see, the most valuable data in the system.

Mechanism · 1 of 4

Whose claims? The vocabulary group

If claims come from institutions, every claim needs to carry whose it is. That is the entire job of the middle entity.

A METADATA record is one vocabulary's statement about one asset. The Getty's tag set is one group, Cleveland's is another, our internal normalized set is a third — all attached to the same artwork, each internally coherent, none overwriting the others.

Why groups are never shared between assets

A group is a specific institution's statement about a specific work. Sharing one across two assets would mean two different works had literally the same claim — which is not what we mean. Assets relate through matching values, not through shared groups.

Mechanism · 2 of 4

Individuation is data too

Return to the devi. Are Durga and Kali one subject or two? The system does not answer — and does not have to, because AssetID identifies a record, not a referent. Nothing in the schema claims an ASSET row corresponds to exactly one thing in the world.

So both readings can exist at once:

  • a merged asset carrying name = Durga and name = Kali, with the traditions that treat them as one attached to it;
  • two separate assets, one name each, with the traditions that hold them distinct attached to each.

Three records, no contradiction, because none of them asserts exclusivity. Curators argue in the data rather than before it.

And they stay connected

The merged record shares name = Durga with one separate record and name = Kali with the other. Traversal finds all three as a cluster without anyone declaring the relationship — the competing individuations are themselves discoverable.

What the system declines to know

Nothing distinguishes "two records, competing views of one subject" from "two records, genuinely different subjects." That is a discovery question answered by traversal, never a constraint. The design consistently refuses to encode an answer it would have to guess.

Mechanism · 3 of 4

Every claim has the same shape

The standard mapping turns a multivalued attribute into its own relation. With an open attribute set, that gives you an open set of relations:

ASSET_ARTIST(AssetID, Artist)
ASSET_PERIOD(AssetID, Period)
ASSET_MEDIUM(AssetID, Medium)
… one more every time a vocabulary arrives

Look closely and they are the same relation three times: a key back to the asset, a name, a value. Once you see that, you collapse them.

METATAG(MetaTagID, MetaDataID, TagName, Value)
One relation for every attribute of every asset. The attribute's name becomes data.

Multivalued falls out for free

Three names for one asset is three rows sharing a TagName. Nothing special is required — the list is the base case, not the exception. This is what lets a work belong to many schools at once.

Mechanism · 4 of 4

The ER diagram

Four entity types, three relationships, no many-to-many anywhere. Every double line marks total participation. ASSET is a record handle, not a claim about what exists.

ShortName is a second candidate key — unique, but AssetID is the primary key, so only AssetID is underlined. Every entity also carries a creation timestamp, omitted here to keep the chain readable.

Mapping

Four relations, unbounded attributes

The 7-step mapping applies unchanged. Three 1:N relationships become three foreign keys on the N side. Nothing exotic happens:

SOURCE(SourceID, Name)
ASSET(AssetID, ShortName, SourceID, ExternalAssetID)
METADATA(MetaDataID, AssetID)
METATAG(MetaTagID, MetaDataID, TagName, Value)
Underline marks the primary key; brown marks a foreign key.

"Internal" is a real source row

Locally captured work is not a NULL special case — the file system is a source like the Getty is a source. Every asset resolves by the same path, and no federation query needs three-valued logic.

ExternalAssetID means nothing alone

It identifies the asset at that source, so it is only meaningful paired with SourceID. That pairing is exactly what the Provides relationship supplies.

A design choice worth defending

One path from tag to asset

METATAG carries no AssetID. Every traversal goes through the group — and that restraint buys two things.

  • Re-pointing is atomic. Changing one METADATA row relocates its entire cluster of tags. One row update moves a hundred claims; a denormalized AssetID would make that a hundred updates.
  • There is one source of truth. A duplicated AssetID can disagree with the group it hangs from. Nothing in the schema could then say which is right.

The honest counterpoint

You are trained to denormalize for read speed, and this is a case where that instinct is wrong. The extra join is cheap and indexed. The second source of truth is not.

The payoff · 1 of 2

Shared values are edges

Two assets that carry the same (TagName, Value) pair are connected by that fact. Nobody declared the relationship — it is discovered. Select a tag to light the assets that share it.

Four assets from three sources. Select a tag above to see which assets share it and the edges that result.

A graph embedded in a relational database — compact, indexed, and queryable with ordinary SQL.

The payoff · 2 of 2

From artworks to institutions

Every asset has exactly one source. So the source is a property of each node in that graph — which means the graph can be aggregated by institution.

Edges between assets become edges between museums, weighted by how much vocabulary their holdings actually share. You can measure that two collections overlap heavily on period but diverge on school.

The point

Measured from what they hold — not from what their public pages claim about themselves.

Nothing was designed for this

No part of the schema anticipates institutional comparison. It falls out because claims, vocabulary, and provenance all live in the same uniform structure. Uniformity is what makes questions possible that nobody modeled for.

Simpler queries use the same machinery: find every medium whose value contains steel, with no type system to fight and no column to have declared in advance.

How discovery actually runs

The fan-out is a graph problem

Finding every commonality cluster by repeated self-joins in SQL would be expensive. So we don't do it in SQL.

Why the projection is free

METATAG is already an edge list — two assets sharing a (TagName, Value) pair are an edge. That is a graph engine's native input, so handing it over is a scan, not a transformation. Connected components or community detection then finds every cluster in one pass, rather than one self-join per question.

And the results become claims

Cluster assignments are written back as ordinary tags, so a clustering run is just another source and its output just another vocabulary group — adopt it or ignore it exactly like a museum's attribution. Storage is cheap, so materializing them costs nothing that matters.

Tuning

Performance is an index question

The schema has one hot table, so speed is mostly a question of which indexes sit on METATAG — and they are cheap in the currency this system spends, so you build several.

METATAG (TagName, Value)
METATAG (TagName, Value, MetaDataID)
METATAG (MetaDataID, TagName)
METATAG (Value)
METADATA (AssetID)
ASSET (SourceID, ExternalAssetID)
Each line is an access path the workload actually uses.
  • (TagName, Value) is the discovery index — every "find everything where medium = steel" is one range scan on it.
  • Adding MetaDataID makes that index covering: the query is answered from the index alone, never touching the table.
  • (MetaDataID, TagName) walks the other direction — pull one group's attributes, or one named attribute of a group.
  • (Value) alone covers search that does not know the attribute name.

A covering index duplicates columns to avoid reading the table: more storage for less I/O — the whole strategy, at the physical level.

Why Value stays bounded

A bounded VARCHAR keeps rows packing cleanly into pages and index keys short — high fanout, a shallow tree, two or three page reads whatever the corpus size. Anything long is a link: TagName = documentlink, Value = the URI. The note then becomes an asset in its own right, with its own provenance. The real trade is a reference that can break with nothing to detect it — so link-checking becomes one more sweep, reporting back as tags.

And JSON is an output, not a storage choice

One grouped traversal with JSON_OBJECTAGG emits the document view whenever anyone wants it. Storing rows costs nothing in representation; storing documents costs the index everything runs on — and leaves no edge list to cluster.

Extension · 1 of 2 — designed for, not yet built

Where correctness lives: views

If the database never adjudicates, who decides what is correct? The answer is: whoever is reading, at the moment they read.

A view is a signed declaration that a particular reading — this individuation, these claims — is the one its notarizer stands behind. Many views can exist over the same data, and an end user chooses which standard to treat as correct.

Nothing new is required

A view is just another record whose tags point at other records. A claim about claims, stored exactly like a claim about a sculpture — which is the strongest evidence that the uniform shape was the right call.

The separation this achieves

No adjudication at write time; correctness selected at read time. The database records what was claimed; the reader supplies the frame.

You have seen this shape already. A database view is a named, reusable perspective over base data — the difference here is that it lives in the data rather than in DDL, so curators create one at runtime instead of a DBA writing a migration.

And because a view records the criteria rather than just the verdict, it is an executable specification — something that can be applied to material its author never saw. The next slide is what that makes possible.

This layer is not built yet. It is on this slide because the architecture already supports it without a schema change.

Extension · 2 of 2 — designed for, not yet built

A reference database for an LLM

Posit a hypothesis, then examine as much material as possible against it. That is already what curators and archivists do — the limit has never been the method, only how much one person can read.

What this store gives RAG that chunks don't

Retrieval returns attributed claims, not anonymous passages — provenance is structural rather than reconstructed. And because conflicts survive, the model can reason about a disagreement instead of being handed a dataset where the dispute has been quietly settled.

It applies the curator's method, not its own

Hand it a curator's preferred view and it executes their criteria across a corpus no one could read — so the methodology itself becomes testable, and two schools' criteria can be run over the same material and their disagreement measured. The model supplies reach and consistency; the scholar supplies the method. Extraction and analysis stay separate sources, and every extracted tag carries document and locus, so any of it can be checked.

Judgment

Where the cost goes

Every design pays somewhere. This one pays in storage and keeps compute cheap — a deliberate choice, not a side effect.

Paid in storage

  • A trip tag written once per asset — 300 times for 300 panoramas.
  • Parallel individuations duplicate whole claim sets.
  • Indexes over a very large METATAG are themselves substantial.
  • Nothing is ever deleted; views would add more records still.

Kept off compute

  • Lookups are index range scans on (TagName, Value).
  • Joins are key equality down a 1:N chain — no full scans.
  • Point lookups never need a traversal; wide fan-out is one graph pass, not a self-join per question.
  • A new federated source in hours, with no schema migration.

Why the trade is free here

One panoramic collection is 1.5 GB; the archive is 3.3 TB of media. Against that, the whole metadata store — duplication, indexes and all — is rounding error. The redundancy that sounds alarming in the abstract is not detectable here.

What is left over

Nothing computational. A graph engine to operate, and materialized clusters that go stale as assets arrive — so the open question is sweep cadence, not query cost.

Wrap-up

Recap & connections

Digital humanities scholarship is built on disagreement. This design records it rather than forcing the field into a model that discards it — a compact glue database where the conflicts live.

The argument

  1. Some domains have no single truth — identity itself can be undefined, and a conventional schema cannot express that.
  2. Claims cluster by whose vocabulary they are — that is METADATA.
  3. Individuation is data too: competing readings coexist as records.
  4. One uniform (TagName, Value) shape for every claim.
  5. Shared pairs are edges — a graph embedded in SQL.
  6. One source per asset lifts the graph to institutions.
  7. Correctness is a view the reader chooses, not a write-time constraint.
