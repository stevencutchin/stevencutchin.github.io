Design judgment

The EAV antipattern
When rows become columns

A design that stores the name of an attribute as data rather than declaring it in the schema. Famous, common, usually wrong — and occasionally the only thing that works.

The goal here is not to forbid it. It is to let you recognize it, state its costs precisely, and defend a decision either way.

Motivation

Where disagreement is the data

Everything in this course so far assumes two things: that you know your attributes, and that each one has a right value. Business systems earn both assumptions — somebody has the authority to declare what an order total is.

The digital humanities have neither. Consider:

  • A manuscript dated to the 11th century by one paleographer and the 13th by another, on competing evidence.
  • Authorship contested for three hundred years, with no prospect of settlement.
  • Prosopography — is the Johannes in this charter the same Johannes as in that one? Often genuinely unknowable.
  • A place with five names across four languages and three periods, none of them the "correct" one.
  • Contested provenance, where the disagreement itself carries legal and ethical weight.

The inversion

In a business system, conflicting records are an error to reconcile. In humanities scholarship, who claimed what, when, and on what evidence is the research object itself. Resolving the conflict destroys the thing being studied.

And this field gets the least help

Relational modeling grew up serving transactions, where one party has authority to declare the answer. Humanities projects have no such authority and the smallest budgets, so they are most often handed tooling designed for inventory. TEI's apparatus for variant readings and CIDOC-CRM for cultural heritage exist precisely because the ordinary approach does not fit.

Definition

The shape

Three columns carry every fact in the database:

ATTRIBUTE(AttrID, EntityID, AttrName, Value)
Entity — which thing. Attribute — which property, as a string. Value — the property's value, almost always as text.

The schema no longer says what a product has. The data says it. Adding a property is an INSERT, not an ALTER TABLE — which is precisely the appeal, and precisely the problem.

The one-sentence test

If a column holds the name of a field, you are looking at EAV.

The field name has moved from the schema into a column.

Recognition

Where you've already met it

EAV is not exotic. You have almost certainly used software built on it this week.

  • WordPress. The postmeta table is (post_id, meta_key, meta_value) — textbook EAV, powering a large share of the web.
  • E-commerce catalogs. Magento's product model is the best-known commercial example; products across categories share almost no specifications.
  • Clinical and laboratory systems. Observation stores hold thousands of possible measurements, any patient having a handful.
  • SaaS custom fields. Any product where the customer defines their own fields is storing those values somewhere, and it is usually here.
  • RDF and triple stores. Subject–predicate–object is EAV done deliberately, with a standard and a query language on top.

Disguises

Columns named field_1 through field_40, a properties text column holding delimited pairs, or a "flexible" audit table — same idea, fewer of the benefits.

The appeal

Why people reach for it

Real problems it answers

  • Irreducible disagreement. One attribute carries several conflicting values, each needing attribution, none allowed to win. A column can hold exactly one value — this is a structural impossibility, not an inconvenience.
  • Unknowable attributes. The set is not yours to know — it arrives from customers, partners, or external systems.
  • Extreme sparsity. Nine hundred possible columns, twenty used per row.
  • Runtime extension. Users add fields without a migration, a deploy, or a DBA.
  • Heterogeneous entities. A laptop and a bedsheet share a catalog and almost no properties.

Bad reasons that look like good ones

  • "We might need more fields later." You will — that is what ALTER TABLE is for.
  • "Migrations are painful here." Fix the deployment process, not the data model.
  • "It is more flexible." Flexibility is not free, and this slide deck is mostly about the bill.
  • "We do not know the domain yet." Then you are not ready to design a schema; EAV will not supply the understanding.

The distinguishing question

Can you, in principle, enumerate the attributes — or does someone outside your control invent them? Only the second is a real case for EAV.

The bill · 1 of 3

Cost 1: the constraints you lose

Every guarantee the DBMS was giving you moves into application code, where it is optional and unenforced.

  • No types. price and material share one VARCHAR. Nothing stops price = 'tomorrow'.
  • No NOT NULL. A required attribute is a missing row, and a missing row looks exactly like an attribute that does not apply.
  • No CHECK, no domains. No range on a measurement, no enumeration on a status.
  • No foreign key from a value. If material should reference a materials table, it cannot.
  • No defense against typos. color and colour are two different attributes, silently, forever.

The underrated one

A wide table's NULL at least records that the column exists and is empty. In EAV, "we never measured it" and "it does not apply" and "someone forgot" are the same absence.

The bill · 2 of 3

Cost 2: queries get worse

Reconstructing one logical row of n attributes costs n joins, or a pivot:

-- EAV: one product, three fields
SELECT p.id, p.name,
  MAX(CASE WHEN a.attr_name='price'
       THEN a.value END) AS price,
  MAX(CASE WHEN a.attr_name='screen_in'
       THEN a.value END) AS screen_in
FROM product p
JOIN product_attr a ON a.entity_id = p.id
WHERE p.id = 2
GROUP BY p.id, p.name;
-- Wide table: the same answer
SELECT id, name, price, screen_in
FROM product WHERE id = 2;

The string-comparison trap

Values live in a text column, so WHERE value > '500' compares strings. On the catalog two slides back it returns the $899 phone and the $89 bedsheet, and misses the $1299 laptop — wrong in both directions at once. You must cast, and a cast in a predicate usually discards the index you were counting on.

  • Sorting by an attribute requires joining to it first.
  • Two-attribute filters mean two joins against the same table.
  • Reporting tools that expect columns cannot read it without a view per entity type.

The bill · 3 of 3

Cost 3: the optimizer goes blind

This is the cost students miss, and it grows with the table rather than staying constant.

  • Statistics stop meaning anything. The planner keeps histograms per column. One value column mixing prices, dates, and materials produces a distribution that describes nothing.
  • Cardinality estimates go wrong. The planner cannot know that attr_name = 'price' selects a different fraction than attr_name = 'material', so it guesses — and a wrong guess at the bottom of a plan multiplies all the way up.
  • Every entity read is a range scan. One row becomes n index entries plus n lookups, where a wide table had a single page fetch.
  • Index bloat. You will need several composite indexes on a table holding every fact in the system, and each one is substantial.

Why it is worth naming

The constraint losses are visible on day one. This cost arrives in year two, when the table is large and the query that was fast in development is not.

Alternatives · 1 of 2

What to try first

Most situations that look like they need EAV are better served by something less drastic.

  • Just add the columns. Sparsity is cheaper than you think — modern engines store NULLs compactly, and sixty mostly-empty columns beat EAV for almost every query you will write.
  • Class-table inheritance. A shared PRODUCT table plus LAPTOP, PHONE, and BEDDING tables for the specifics. You met this as the EER specialization mapping in Unit 2 — it is the right answer whenever the entity types are known and few.
  • The hybrid. Real columns for the attributes everything has — price, name, dates, anything you filter or sort on — and an EAV tail only for the genuinely open remainder. This is usually the correct design when EAV is tempting at all.

The hybrid deserves emphasis

Pure EAV is rarely necessary. If even a third of your attributes are known and queried, pulling those into columns recovers most of what EAV costs you while keeping the flexibility where it is needed.

Alternatives · 2 of 2

The modern competitor: JSON columns

The real decision today is rarely EAV versus wide tables. It is EAV versus a document column on the row:

ALTER TABLE product
  ADD specs JSON;

-- MySQL: index a path via a generated column
ALTER TABLE product
  ADD material VARCHAR(40)
    AS (specs->>'$.material') STORED,
  ADD INDEX (material);

PostgreSQL goes further: JSONB with a GIN index searches arbitrary keys without declaring each one.

The decision rule

Ask which query dominates. "Give me everything about entity N" favors JSON — the whole record is one page fetch. "Find every entity where some attribute equals X", across the whole corpus, favors EAV — those are rows, and rows join and index naturally.

JSON keeps the entity together and loses the cross-entity view. EAV scatters the entity and gains it. Neither restores type safety or constraints — on that, both are equally undeclared.

The diagnostic

When EAV is actually right

Four questions. If you do not answer all four in EAV's favor, use something from the previous two slides.

  1. Who controls the vocabulary? If you do, declare it. EAV earns its place only when attributes arrive from systems or people you do not control.
  2. Which way do you query? Mostly across entities hunting for attribute values, rather than pulling one entity's full record.
  3. Who enforces correctness? There must be an application layer that already owns validation, because the database will not be doing it.
  4. Is the openness essential or convenient? If a migration would solve it, a migration should solve it.

Where all four line up

Digital humanities projects recording contested scholarship; federated catalogs ingesting external vocabularies; clinical observation stores; scientific instrument data; customer-defined fields in multi-tenant SaaS; and RDF triple stores, which are EAV promoted to a standard.

In the wild

A worked case

OpenPipe — a federated art-collection pipeline built here at Boise State — answers all four questions in EAV's favor:

  • Vocabularies arrive from the Getty, the Cleveland Museum of Art, and individual curators. Nobody at Boise State controls them.
  • The dominant query is cross-entity: find everything sharing a value, and discover which holdings connect.
  • Validation and signing live in the application layer.
  • The openness is essential — the whole point is recording conflicting claims rather than adjudicating them.

What it trades

Storage, deliberately. Duplication and indexes are rounding error against terabytes of captured media, and every discovery query stays a simple index range scan.

Note the direction of the argument

The case is made from the domain's requirements toward the design — not from the design toward a justification. That is what separates a defensible EAV from a lazy one.

Wrap-up

Recap & check yourself

Today

  • EAV stores the name of a field as data.
  • It buys an open attribute set and runtime extension.
  • It costs types, constraints, query simplicity, and the optimizer's ability to plan.
  • Try columns, class-table inheritance, a hybrid, or a JSON column first.
  • It is right when the vocabulary is not yours and the queries run across entities.

Check yourself

  1. Why does WHERE value > '500' return the wrong rows, and what does fixing it cost?
  2. A table has columns field_1 … field_30. Is it EAV? What does it share, and what does it lack?
  3. Your catalog has 40 attributes; 8 are on every product and queried constantly. What do you build?
  4. Name a case where a JSON column beats EAV, and one where it loses.
